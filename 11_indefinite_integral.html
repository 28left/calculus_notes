14  The Indefinite Integral

NoteCompanion reading

This lecture corresponds to C&J §2.4 (the integral as a function of the upper limit).

So far we have treated the integral \(\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\) (Definition 11.2) as a number attached to a function and an interval. But this number depends on the limits of integration \(a\) and \(b\). If we hold the lower limit fixed and let the upper limit vary, the integral becomes a function of that upper limit — and it is worth asking what kind of function we get.

14.1 Definition and Basic Formula

To make clear that the upper limit is now the variable, we rename it \(x\) and use a different letter, say \(u\), for the variable of integration. (Recall that the name of the integration variable is irrelevant: \(\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = \int_a^b f(u)\,du\).)

Definition 14.1
Let \(f\) be continuous on an interval \(I\), and let \(\alpha\in I\). The function \[ \Phi_\alpha(x) = \int_\alpha^x f(u)\,du \qquad (x\in I) \] is called an indefinite integral of \(f\). For \(x<\alpha\) and \(x=\alpha\) the integral is understood in the sense of Definition 13.1; in particular \(\Phi_\alpha(\alpha)=0\).

Remark 14.1. Note the article: an indefinite integral, not the indefinite integral. If we choose a different lower limit \(\beta\), we obtain a different indefinite integral, \[ \Phi_\beta(x) = \int_\beta^x f(u)\,du. \] As we will see in a moment, the choice of the lower limit is not that important. So we fix \(\alpha\) once and for all and simply write \[ \Phi(x) = \Phi_\alpha(x). \]

Proposition 14.1 (Basic properties of the indefinite integral) Let \(f\) be continuous on \(I\) and \(\Phi(x) = \int_\alpha^x f(u)\,du\).

  1. If we know \(\Phi\), we can compute any definite integral of \(f\): for all \(a,b\in I\), \[ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = \Phi(b) - \Phi(a). \]
  2. Any other indefinite integral differs from \(\Phi\) by a constant: for every \(\beta\in I\), \[ \Phi_\beta(x) = \int_\beta^x f(u)\,du = \Phi(x) - \Phi(\beta). \]
NoteProof

Proof.

  1. By additivity of the integral (Proposition 13.1, in the general form of Exercise 13.1, since \(a\), \(b\), \(\alpha\) may lie in any order) and by Definition 13.1, \[ \begin{aligned} \int_a^b f(x)\,dx &= \int_a^\alpha f(x)\,dx + \int_\alpha^b f(x)\,dx \\ &= -\int_\alpha^a f(x)\,dx + \int_\alpha^b f(x)\,dx \\ &= \Phi(b) - \Phi(a). \end{aligned} \]

  2. This is the special case \(a = \beta\), \(b = x\) of (1).

Remark 14.2. In (2) the constant \(-\Phi(\beta)\) depends on \(\beta\), but not on \(x\). So all the indefinite integrals of \(f\) have graphs that are vertical translates of one another; changing the lower limit only shifts the graph up or down. Part (1) generalizes Corollary 13.1: there the lower limit \(a\) played the role of \(\alpha\).

14.2 Properties of \(\Phi\) as a Function

What properties does \(\Phi(x)\) have as a function? Geometrically, \(\Phi(x)\) is the signed area (Remark 13.1) under the graph of \(f\) between \(\alpha\) and \(x\), and moving \(x\) to the right adds the signed area over the new piece of the interval.

Proposition 14.2

  • If \(f\ge 0\) on \(I\), then \(\Phi\) is increasing (Definition 8.3): as \(x\) gets bigger we add positive area.
  • If \(f\le 0\) on \(I\), then \(\Phi\) is decreasing: as \(x\) gets bigger we add negative area.
NoteProof

Proof. Let \(x<y\) in \(I\) (for \(x=y\) there is nothing to show). By Proposition 14.1, \[ \Phi(y) - \Phi(x) = \int_x^y f(u)\,du. \] If \(f\ge 0\), this integral is \(\ge 0\) by Proposition 13.5, so \(\Phi(x)\le\Phi(y)\). If \(f\le 0\), apply Proposition 13.5 to \(-f\ge0\) and use Proposition 13.3 to get \(\int_x^y f(u)\,du\le 0\), so \(\Phi(x)\ge\Phi(y)\).

Continuity of the indefinite integral

Theorem 14.1
If \(f\) is continuous on \(I\), then \(\Phi\) is continuous on \(I\).

NoteProof

Proof. Let \(x, y\in I\). By Proposition 14.1 and the Mean Value Theorem for Integrals (Theorem 13.1, which requires \(f\) continuous; for \(y<x\) use Remark 13.4, part 3), \[ \Phi(y) - \Phi(x) = \int_x^y f(u)\,du = f(c)\,(y-x) \qquad (\ast) \] for some \(c\) between \(x\) and \(y\).

What happens if \(y\to x\)? For \(\Phi\) to be continuous at \(x\), we need \(\Phi(y)\to\Phi(x)\) (Proposition 10.2). Using \((\ast)\) and the limit laws (Proposition 10.3), \[ \begin{aligned} \lim_{y\to x}\Phi(y) &= \lim_{y\to x}\bigl[\Phi(x) + f(c)\,(y-x)\bigr] \\ &= \lim_{y\to x}\Phi(x) + \lim_{y\to x} f(c)\,(y-x) \\ &= \Phi(x) + f(x)\cdot 0 = \Phi(x). \end{aligned} \]

Remark 14.3. The point \(c\) in \((\ast)\) depends on \(y\), so a little care is needed in the last step. Since \(c\) lies between \(x\) and \(y\), we have \(c\to x\) as \(y\to x\), and hence \(f(c)\to f(x)\) by continuity of \(f\). In fact, all we really need is that \(f(c)\) stays bounded while \(y-x\to 0\) — which is exactly what the next result exploits.

Proposition 14.3 (Lipschitz continuity) Let \(f\) be continuous on \(I\), let \([a,b]\subseteq I\), and let \(M = \max_{[a,b]}\lvert f \rvert\) (this maximum exists by the Extreme Value Theorem, see Remark 11.2). Then for all \(x, y\in[a,b]\), \[ \lvert \Phi(y) - \Phi(x) \rvert \le M\,\lvert y-x \rvert. \] A function satisfying an estimate of this kind is called Lipschitz continuous.

NoteProof

Proof. By \((\ast)\), \(\lvert \Phi(y)-\Phi(x) \rvert = \lvert f(c) \rvert\,\lvert y-x \rvert\) with \(c\) between \(x\) and \(y\), so \(c\in[a,b]\) and \(\lvert f(c) \rvert\le M\).

Remark 14.4. Lipschitz continuity is a stronger property than continuity: given \(\varepsilon>0\), the choice \(\delta = \varepsilon/(M+1)\) works at every point of \([a,b]\) simultaneously (compare Example 6.3, where the same kind of \(\delta\) appeared). In other words, \(\Phi\) is uniformly continuous on \([a,b]\) in the sense of Equation 11.1, and here we get this directly, with an explicit \(\delta\).

14.3 Examples: Volumes and Dipsticks

Indefinite integrals arise naturally whenever a quantity accumulates as a variable grows. A good example is the volume of liquid in a container, as a function of the fill height.

Suppose a container has a vertical axis, and let \(A(u)\) be the area of its horizontal cross-section at height \(u\) above the bottom. Slicing the container into thin horizontal layers of thickness \(\Delta_i\), the volume up to height \(x\) is approximately \(\sum_i A(u_i^*)\,\Delta_i\) — a Riemann sum (Definition 11.1) for \(A\) on \([0,x]\). Letting the layers get thinner, we obtain the volume as an indefinite integral of \(A\) with lower limit \(\alpha = 0\): \[ V(x) = \int_0^x A(u)\,du. \]

A cone-shaped glass

Example 14.1 (Constructing a dipstick) A glass has the shape of an (upside-down) cone with rim radius \(R\) and height \(H\). At which height \(h\) is the glass half full?

The cross-section at height \(u\) is a disk. Its radius grows linearly from \(0\) at the bottom to \(R\) at the rim, so it equals \(R\cdot\frac{u}{H}\), and \[ A(u) = \pi\Big(\frac{R\,u}{H}\Big)^2 = \pi\,\frac{R^2}{H^2}\,u^2 . \] By Proposition 13.3 and Proposition 13.4 (or directly by the Riemann sum computation of \(\int_0^b x^2\,dx\) in the previous chapters), \[ V(x) = \int_0^x A(u)\,du = \pi\,\frac{R^2}{H^2}\int_0^x u^2\,du = \pi\,\frac{R^2}{H^2}\cdot\frac{x^3}{3}. \] In particular, the total volume is \[ V_T = V(H) = \pi\,\frac{R^2}{H^2}\cdot\frac{H^3}{3} = \frac{\pi}{3}R^2H, \] the familiar formula for the volume of a cone.

We want to know for which \(x\) we have \(V(x) = \frac12 V_T\): \[ \pi\,\frac{R^2}{H^2}\cdot\frac{x^3}{3} = \pi\,\frac{R^2}{H^2}\cdot\frac{H^3}{3}\cdot\frac12 \quad \Leftrightarrow \quad x^3 = \frac{H^3}{2} \quad \Leftrightarrow \quad x = \frac{H}{\sqrt[3]{2}} \approx 0.79\,H . \] So the “half full” mark on the dipstick sits at about \(79\%\) of the height, not at \(50\%\). Indeed, filling the glass to half its height gives only \[ V\big(\tfrac12 H\big) = \pi\,\frac{R^2}{H^2}\cdot\frac{H^3}{24} = \frac18\,V(H), \] an eighth of the total volume.

Remark 14.5. Why does a height \(x\) with \(V(x) = \frac12 V_T\) exist? Here we found \(x\) explicitly, but there is a reason that works for any container. By Theorem 14.1, \(V\) is continuous on \([0,H]\). Moreover \(V(0) = 0\) and \(V(H) = V_T\), so \[ V(0) = 0 < \tfrac12 V_T < V_T = V(H). \] By the Intermediate Value Theorem (Theorem 10.2, in the general form of Remark 10.4), there exists \(c\in(0,H)\) with \(V(c) = \frac12 V_T\). The same argument gives a mark for every fraction of the total volume — a dipstick can always be calibrated, even if we cannot compute \(V\) in closed form.

A horizontal oil tank

Example 14.2 (Oil tank) An oil tank is a cylinder of radius \(R\) and length \(L\), buried horizontally in the ground. A measuring stick lowered vertically through the top measures the height \(x\) of the oil in the tank. Reading the stick, how much oil (volume) is left?

(Why not bury the tank vertically? The math would be easier: then every cross-section is a disk of area \(\pi R^2\), and \(V(x) = \pi R^2 x\) is linear in \(x\).)

Step 1: the area of the cross-section, as a function of \(u\). At height \(u\) (with \(0\le u\le 2R\)) the oil surface is a rectangle of length \(L\) and width \(W(u)\), so \(A(u) = L\cdot W(u)\). To find \(W(u)\), look at the circular end of the tank. The center of the circle lies at height \(R\), so the horizontal chord at height \(u\) is at distance \(\lvert R-u \rvert\) from the center. By Pythagoras, half the chord has length \[ r(u) = \sqrt{R^2 - (R-u)^2} = \sqrt{2Ru - u^2} = \sqrt{u\,(2R-u)}, \] and therefore \[ W(u) = 2\sqrt{u\,(2R-u)}, \qquad A(u) = 2L\sqrt{u\,(2R-u)} . \]

Step 2: the volume. Hence the volume at height \(x\) is \[ V(x) = \int_0^x 2L\sqrt{u\,(2R-u)}\,du, \qquad 0\le x\le 2R . \] We do not know how to integrate this function yet — it is not covered by Proposition 13.4. But we can still compute \(V(x)\) as accurately as we like using Riemann sums (Theorem 11.1).

The coral strip is a typical thin slice of a Riemann sum for \(V(x)\). The graph on the right plots the volume \(V(x)\), as a percentage of the full tank, horizontally against the height \(x\) vertically. The orange dashed guides show how the \(25\%\), \(50\%\) and \(75\%\) marks on the dipstick are found: start at the desired volume, go up to the curve, then across to read off the height. This is solving \(V(x) = c\), whose solvability is guaranteed by the argument of Remark 14.5.

Two things can be seen directly in the picture. First, the \(25\%\) and \(75\%\) marks sit closer to the \(50\%\) mark than to the ends of the stick, because the tank is widest in the middle. Second, the volume changes fastest near mid-height, where the cross-section \(A(u)\) is largest (on the graph, the curve is flattest there, since the height is plotted vertically). This is the Lipschitz bound of Remark 14.6 below in action: a misread of the stick costs the most volume where the tank is widest.

Remark 14.6. Some remarks.

  1. \(V(y) - V(x)\) is the total change in volume between heights \(x\) and \(y\), and by Proposition 14.1, \[ V(y) - V(x) = \int_x^y A(u)\,du . \]
  2. Lipschitz continuity of \(V\) (Proposition 14.3) is of practical use. Suppose we botch our measurement. How wrong can our calculated volume at most be? Let \(x_*\) be our measurement and \(x\) the actual height. Then \[ \lvert V(x_*) - V(x) \rvert \le M\,\lvert x_* - x \rvert, \] where \(M = \max_{[0,2R]} A\). So the error in the volume is at most a constant multiple of the error in the measurement. For the oil tank we can even give \(M\) explicitly: since \(u(2R-u) = R^2 - (R-u)^2 \le R^2\), the cross-section is widest at the center height \(u=R\), and \(M = A(R) = 2LR\). So a reading that is off by \(1\,\text{cm}\) misjudges the volume by at most \(2LR\cdot 1\,\text{cm}\) — the volume of a layer \(1\,\text{cm}\) thick through the widest part of the tank.
