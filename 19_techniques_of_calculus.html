23  Product Rule and Integration by Parts

With the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (Theorem 22.2), every derivative we know gives us an antiderivative, and with it a way to evaluate definite integrals (Corollary 22.1). In this chapter we take stock of what we know so far, and then add the first rule that goes beyond sums and constant multiples: the product rule, together with its counterpart for integrals, integration by parts.

Throughout, \(\int f(x)\,dx = F(x)+c\) stands for an arbitrary antiderivative of \(f\), as in Remark 22.4.

23.1 Summary of Elementary Functions

Some comments on the table:

  • The column \(F(x)\) lists an antiderivative of \(f(x)\), plus the arbitrary constant. By Lemma 22.1, these are all the antiderivatives, as long as we work on an interval.
  • Each entry in the column \(F(x)\) can be checked by differentiating it, using the column \(f'(x)\). For instance, \(\big(\tfrac{1}{\alpha+1}x^{\alpha+1}\big)' = x^\alpha\). So an antiderivative is verified as soon as the corresponding derivative is.
  • Constants: \((a)'=0\) because every difference quotient of a constant function is \(0\), and \(\int_a^b c\,dx = c(b-a)\) was computed directly from Riemann sums (Example 11.1).
  • Power functions: we proved \((x^n)' = n x^{n-1}\) for all integers \(n\) (Proposition 16.1, Proposition 16.2), with \(x\neq0\) if \(n<0\). For negative exponents, the antiderivative is only valid on an interval not containing \(0\). Non-integer exponents such as \(x^{1/2}\) or \(x^\pi\) have not been treated in general yet.
  • The formula for \(F\) fails for \(\alpha=-1\), since it would divide by \(0\). The gap is filled by the logarithm: since \((\ln x)' = \frac1x\), an antiderivative of \(x^{-1}\) on \((0,\infty)\) is \(\ln x\) (compare Proposition 13.4).
  • Trigonometric functions: both derivatives were computed from the difference quotient (Proposition 16.3).
  • Exponential and logarithm: the derivatives \((e^x)'=e^x\) and \((\ln x)'=\frac1x\) will be proved later. We already use them in examples. The antiderivative of \(\ln x\) is still open; we will find it with integration by parts (Exercise 23.1).

23.2 Basic Rules

The following rules hold for derivatives and integrals alike.

The rules for derivatives were proved from the difference quotient, the rules for integrals from Riemann sums. They also hold for antiderivatives: if \(F\) and \(G\) are antiderivatives of \(f\) and \(g\), then \(F+G\) is an antiderivative of \(f+g\) and \(\alpha F\) is an antiderivative of \(\alpha f\), by the rules for derivatives. In the indefinite notation, \[ \int \big(f(x)+g(x)\big)\,dx = \int f(x)\,dx + \int g(x)\,dx, \qquad \int \alpha f(x)\,dx = \alpha\int f(x)\,dx . \]

The table of elementary functions together with the basic rules covers many functions, for instance all polynomials and all linear combinations of \(\sin\), \(\cos\) and \(e^x\). But what about products \(f(x)\,g(x)\) and compositions \(f(g(x))\)? We saw in Remark 16.2 and Remark 13.2 that the naive rules for products are false. In this chapter we deal with products. Compositions will be handled by the chain rule and substitution.

23.3 The Product Rule

The trick is to add and subtract the “mixed” term \(f(x)g(a)\) in the numerator of the difference quotient of \(fg\) at \(a\). For \(x\neq a\), \[ \begin{aligned} \frac{f(x)g(x) - f(a)g(a)}{x-a} &= \frac{f(x)g(x) - f(x)g(a) + f(x)g(a) - f(a)g(a)}{x-a} \\[4pt] &= f(x)\,\frac{g(x)-g(a)}{x-a} \;+\; \frac{f(x)-f(a)}{x-a}\,g(a) . \end{aligned} \tag{23.1}\] This is an identity of difference quotients; no limit has been taken yet. As \(x\to a\), the two difference quotients on the right tend to \(g'(a)\) and \(f'(a)\).

Theorem 23.1 (Product rule) If \(f\) and \(g\) are differentiable at \(a\), then so is \(f\cdot g\), and \[ \big(f(x)\,g(x)\big)'\Big|_{x=a} = f'(a)\,g(a) + f(a)\,g'(a). \] Consequently, if \(f\) and \(g\) are differentiable on an interval \(I\), then on \(I\) \[ \big(f(x)\,g(x)\big)' = f'(x)\,g(x) + f(x)\,g'(x). \]

NoteProof

Proof. By Equation 23.1, \[ \begin{aligned} \big(f(x)\,g(x)\big)'\Big|_{x=a} &= \lim_{x\to a}\frac{f(x)g(x)-f(a)g(a)}{x-a} \\[4pt] &= \lim_{x\to a} f(x)\,\frac{g(x)-g(a)}{x-a} \;+\; \lim_{x\to a}\frac{f(x)-f(a)}{x-a}\,g(a) \\[4pt] &= f(a)\,g'(a) + f'(a)\,g(a). \end{aligned} \] Here we used the limit laws (Proposition 10.3) together with three limits: \(\frac{g(x)-g(a)}{x-a}\to g'(a)\) and \(\frac{f(x)-f(a)}{x-a}\to f'(a)\) by definition of the derivative, and \(f(x)\to f(a)\) because \(f\) is differentiable, hence continuous, at \(a\) (Theorem 16.1). Since the limits on the right exist, so does the limit on the left, and the splitting in the second line is justified.

Example 23.1  

  • For \(f(x)=g(x)=x\), the product rule gives \((x\cdot x)' = 1\cdot x + x\cdot 1 = 2x\), in agreement with \((x^2)'=2x\) (compare Remark 16.2).
  • \((x\sin x)' = 1\cdot\sin x + x\cos x = \sin x + x\cos x\).
  • \((x e^x)' = e^x + x e^x = (x+1)\,e^x\).

23.4 Integration by Parts

What does the product rule give for integrals? Let \(f\) and \(g\) be differentiable on an interval. The product rule says that \(f(x)\,g(x)\) is an antiderivative of \(f'(x)g(x)+f(x)g'(x)\), so \[ \begin{aligned} f(x)\,g(x) + c &= \int \big(f'(x)\,g(x) + f(x)\,g'(x)\big)\,dx \\ &= \int f'(x)\,g(x)\,dx + \int f(x)\,g'(x)\,dx , \end{aligned} \] using the sum rule for antiderivatives. Solving for the first integral on the right gives the following rule.

Theorem 23.2 (Integration by parts) Let \(f\) and \(g\) be differentiable on an interval \(I\). Then \[ \int f'(x)\,g(x)\,dx = f(x)\,g(x) - \int f(x)\,g'(x)\,dx + c . \] More precisely: if \(G\) is an antiderivative of \(f\,g'\) on \(I\), then \(f\,g-G\) is an antiderivative of \(f'\,g\) on \(I\).

NoteProof

Proof. By the product rule (Theorem 23.1) and the sum rule (Proposition 16.4), \[ \big(f(x)g(x) - G(x)\big)' = f'(x)g(x) + f(x)g'(x) - f(x)g'(x) = f'(x)\,g(x) . \]

Strictly speaking, the constant \(c\) in the formula is redundant, since \(\int f(x)g'(x)\,dx\) already stands for an arbitrary antiderivative. Writing it out is a useful reminder that the result is only determined up to a constant.

How do we use the formula? It applies when the integrand is a product, one factor of which we read as \(f'(x)\) and the other as \(g(x)\): \[ \underbrace{\int f'(x)\,g(x)\,dx}_{\text{want to compute this}} = f(x)\,g(x) - \int f(x)\,g'(x)\,dx + c . \]

  • We need to know an antiderivative \(f(x)\) of the factor \(f'(x)\).
  • We hope that the new integral \(\int f(x)\,g'(x)\,dx\) is “easier” to compute than the original one. Typically this happens when differentiating \(g\) makes it simpler, as for \(g(x)=x\) or \(g(x)=\ln x\).

Example 23.2
Compute \(\int e^x\,x\,dx\). We choose \[ \begin{aligned} f'(x) &= e^x, & g(x) &= x, \\ f(x) &= e^x, & g'(x) &= 1 . \end{aligned} \] Then by Theorem 23.2, \[ \begin{aligned} \int e^x\,x\,dx &= e^x\,x - \int e^x\cdot 1\,dx + c \\ &= e^x\,x - e^x + c \\ &= e^x\,(x-1) + c . \end{aligned} \] The remaining integral \(\int e^x\,dx = e^x\) is in Table 23.1.

Verification. By the product rule, \[ \frac{d}{dx}\Big(e^x\,(x-1)\Big) = e^x\,(x-1) + e^x\cdot 1 = e^x\,x - e^x + e^x = e^x\,x . \quad\checkmark \]

Remark 23.1. The choice of \(f'\) and \(g\) matters. In Example 23.2 we could also have chosen \(f'(x)=x\) and \(g(x)=e^x\), so \(f(x)=\frac{x^2}{2}\) and \(g'(x)=e^x\). This gives \[ \int x\,e^x\,dx = \frac{x^2}{2}\,e^x - \int \frac{x^2}{2}\,e^x\,dx + c , \] which is correct but useless: the new integral is harder than the original one, because the power of \(x\) went up instead of down.

Combining Theorem 23.2 with the evaluation of definite integrals (Corollary 22.1) gives a version for definite integrals. We write \(\big[h(x)\big]_a^b = h(b)-h(a)\).

Corollary 23.1 (Integration by parts for definite integrals) Let \(f\) and \(g\) be differentiable on an interval containing \([a,b]\), with \(f'\) and \(g'\) continuous. Then \[ \int_a^b f'(x)\,g(x)\,dx = \Big[f(x)\,g(x)\Big]_a^b - \int_a^b f(x)\,g'(x)\,dx . \]

NoteProof

Proof. The functions \(f'g\) and \(fg'\) are continuous, so both integrals exist. By FTC, Part 1 (Theorem 22.1), \(G(x) = \int_a^x f(u)\,g'(u)\,du\) is an antiderivative of \(fg'\), and by Theorem 23.2, \(fg-G\) is an antiderivative of \(f'g\). Now Corollary 22.1 gives \[ \int_a^b f'(x)\,g(x)\,dx = \Big[f(x)g(x) - G(x)\Big]_a^b = \Big[f(x)\,g(x)\Big]_a^b - \big(G(b)-G(a)\big), \] and \(G(b)-G(a) = \int_a^b f(u)\,g'(u)\,du - 0\).

Example 23.3
By Example 23.2, \[ \int_0^1 x\,e^x\,dx = \Big[e^x\,(x-1)\Big]_0^1 = e\cdot 0 - e^0\cdot(-1) = 1 . \]

Exercise 23.1 Fill in the missing entry of Table 23.1: find an antiderivative of \(\ln x\) on \((0,\infty)\), and verify your answer by differentiating. (Hint: write \(\ln x = 1\cdot\ln x\) and take \(f'(x)=1\).)

NoteSolution

Solution. With \(f'(x)=1\), \(g(x)=\ln x\), we have \(f(x)=x\) and \(g'(x)=\frac1x\), so \[ \int \ln x\,dx = x\ln x - \int x\cdot\frac1x\,dx + c = x\ln x - \int 1\,dx + c = x\ln x - x + c . \] Check, using the product rule: \(\big(x\ln x - x\big)' = 1\cdot\ln x + x\cdot\frac1x - 1 = \ln x\).

23.5 Summary

  • Table 23.1 collects the derivatives and antiderivatives of the elementary functions. Those for constants, integer powers, \(\sin\) and \(\cos\) have been proved; those for \(e^x\) and \(\ln x\) will be proved later. An antiderivative is verified by differentiating it.
  • The basic rules (Table 23.2), constant multiple, sum rule and additivity, hold for derivatives and integrals, and linearity also holds for antiderivatives.
  • Product rule (Theorem 23.1): \((fg)' = f'g + fg'\). The proof adds and subtracts \(f(x)g(a)\) in the difference quotient (Equation 23.1) and uses that a differentiable function is continuous.
  • Integration by parts (Theorem 23.2) is the product rule read backwards: \(\int f'g\,dx = fg - \int fg'\,dx + c\). It is useful when we know an antiderivative of \(f'\) and the new integral \(\int fg'\,dx\) is easier, as for \(\int x e^x\,dx = e^x(x-1)+c\) (Example 23.2). The choice of \(f'\) and \(g\) matters (Remark 23.1).
  • For definite integrals, \(\int_a^b f'g\,dx = \big[fg\big]_a^b - \int_a^b fg'\,dx\) (Corollary 23.1).
