14  The Indefinite Integral

NoteCompanion reading

This lecture corresponds to C&J §2.4 (the integral as a function of the upper limit).

So far we have treated the integral \(\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\) (Definition 11.2) as a number attached to a function and an interval. But this number depends on the limits of integration \(a\) and \(b\). If we hold the lower limit fixed and let the upper limit vary, the integral becomes a function of that upper limit — and it is worth asking what kind of function we get.

14.1 Definition and Basic Formula

To make clear that the upper limit is now the variable, we rename it \(x\) and use a different letter, say \(u\), for the variable of integration. (Recall that the name of the integration variable is irrelevant: \(\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = \int_a^b f(u)\,du\).)

Definition 14.1
Let \(f\) be continuous on an interval \(I\), and let \(\alpha\in I\). The function \[ \Phi_\alpha(x) = \int_\alpha^x f(u)\,du \qquad (x\in I) \] is called an indefinite integral of \(f\). For \(x<\alpha\) and \(x=\alpha\) the integral is understood in the sense of Definition 13.1; in particular \(\Phi_\alpha(\alpha)=0\).

Remark 14.1. Note the article: an indefinite integral, not the indefinite integral. If we choose a different lower limit \(\beta\), we obtain a different indefinite integral, \[ \Phi_\beta(x) = \int_\beta^x f(u)\,du. \] As we will see in a moment, the choice of the lower limit is not that important. So we fix \(\alpha\) once and for all and simply write \[ \Phi(x) = \Phi_\alpha(x). \]

Proposition 14.1 (Basic properties of the indefinite integral) Let \(f\) be continuous on \(I\) and \(\Phi(x) = \int_\alpha^x f(u)\,du\).

  1. If we know \(\Phi\), we can compute any definite integral of \(f\): for all \(a,b\in I\), \[ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = \Phi(b) - \Phi(a). \]
  2. Any other indefinite integral differs from \(\Phi\) by a constant: for every \(\beta\in I\), \[ \Phi_\beta(x) = \int_\beta^x f(u)\,du = \Phi(x) - \Phi(\beta). \]
NoteProof

Proof.

  1. By additivity of the integral (Proposition 13.1, in the general form of Exercise 13.1, since \(a\), \(b\), \(\alpha\) may lie in any order) and by Definition 13.1, \[ \begin{aligned} \int_a^b f(x)\,dx &= \int_a^\alpha f(x)\,dx + \int_\alpha^b f(x)\,dx \\ &= -\int_\alpha^a f(x)\,dx + \int_\alpha^b f(x)\,dx \\ &= \Phi(b) - \Phi(a). \end{aligned} \]

  2. This is the special case \(a = \beta\), \(b = x\) of (1).

Remark 14.2. In (2) the constant \(-\Phi(\beta)\) depends on \(\beta\), but not on \(x\). So all the indefinite integrals of \(f\) have graphs that are vertical translates of one another; changing the lower limit only shifts the graph up or down. Part (1) generalizes Corollary 13.1: there the lower limit \(a\) played the role of \(\alpha\).

14.2 Properties of \(\Phi\) as a Function

What properties does \(\Phi(x)\) have as a function? Geometrically, \(\Phi(x)\) is the signed area (Remark 13.1) under the graph of \(f\) between \(\alpha\) and \(x\), and moving \(x\) to the right adds the signed area over the new piece of the interval.

Proposition 14.2

  • If \(f\ge 0\) on \(I\), then \(\Phi\) is increasing (Definition 8.3): as \(x\) gets bigger we add positive area.
  • If \(f\le 0\) on \(I\), then \(\Phi\) is decreasing: as \(x\) gets bigger we add negative area.
NoteProof

Proof. Let \(x<y\) in \(I\) (for \(x=y\) there is nothing to show). By Proposition 14.1, \[ \Phi(y) - \Phi(x) = \int_x^y f(u)\,du. \] If \(f\ge 0\), this integral is \(\ge 0\) by Proposition 13.5, so \(\Phi(x)\le\Phi(y)\). If \(f\le 0\), apply Proposition 13.5 to \(-f\ge0\) and use Proposition 13.3 to get \(\int_x^y f(u)\,du\le 0\), so \(\Phi(x)\ge\Phi(y)\).

Continuity of the indefinite integral

Theorem 14.1
If \(f\) is continuous on \(I\), then \(\Phi\) is continuous on \(I\).

NoteProof

Proof. Let \(x, y\in I\). By Proposition 14.1 and the Mean Value Theorem for Integrals (Theorem 13.1, which requires \(f\) continuous; for \(y<x\) use Remark 13.4, part 3), \[ \Phi(y) - \Phi(x) = \int_x^y f(u)\,du = f(c)\,(y-x) \qquad (\ast) \] for some \(c\) between \(x\) and \(y\).

What happens if \(y\to x\)? For \(\Phi\) to be continuous at \(x\), we need \(\Phi(y)\to\Phi(x)\) (Proposition 10.2). Using \((\ast)\) and the limit laws (Proposition 10.3), \[ \begin{aligned} \lim_{y\to x}\Phi(y) &= \lim_{y\to x}\bigl[\Phi(x) + f(c)\,(y-x)\bigr] \\ &= \lim_{y\to x}\Phi(x) + \lim_{y\to x} f(c)\,(y-x) \\ &= \Phi(x) + f(x)\cdot 0 = \Phi(x). \end{aligned} \]

Remark 14.3. The point \(c\) in \((\ast)\) depends on \(y\), so a little care is needed in the last step. Since \(c\) lies between \(x\) and \(y\), we have \(c\to x\) as \(y\to x\), and hence \(f(c)\to f(x)\) by continuity of \(f\). In fact, all we really need is that \(f(c)\) stays bounded while \(y-x\to 0\) — which is exactly what the next result exploits.

Proposition 14.3 (Lipschitz continuity) Let \(f\) be continuous on \(I\), let \([a,b]\subseteq I\), and let \(M = \max_{[a,b]}\lvert f \rvert\) (this maximum exists by the Extreme Value Theorem, see Remark 11.2). Then for all \(x, y\in[a,b]\), \[ \lvert \Phi(y) - \Phi(x) \rvert \le M\,\lvert y-x \rvert. \] A function satisfying an estimate of this kind is called Lipschitz continuous.

NoteProof

Proof. By \((\ast)\), \(\lvert \Phi(y)-\Phi(x) \rvert = \lvert f(c) \rvert\,\lvert y-x \rvert\) with \(c\) between \(x\) and \(y\), so \(c\in[a,b]\) and \(\lvert f(c) \rvert\le M\).

Remark 14.4. Lipschitz continuity is a stronger property than continuity: given \(\varepsilon>0\), the choice \(\delta = \varepsilon/(M+1)\) works at every point of \([a,b]\) simultaneously (compare Example 6.3, where the same kind of \(\delta\) appeared). In other words, \(\Phi\) is uniformly continuous on \([a,b]\) in the sense of Equation 11.1, and here we get this directly, with an explicit \(\delta\).
