18  The Mean Value Theorem

18.1 Local and Global Information

The derivative \[ f'(x) = \lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h} \] is a local quantity: it only depends on the values of \(f\) in an arbitrarily small neighborhood of \(x\). A difference quotient \[ \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}, \] on the other hand, compares the values of \(f\) at two points that may be far apart, so it reflects a global property of \(f\). The Mean Value Theorem connects the two. It is the most important tool for deriving global properties of a function from local properties of its derivative.

Geometric picture. The difference quotient \(\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}\) is the slope of the secant through the points \((a,f(a))\) and \((b,f(b))\) of the graph of \(f\). Imagine shifting this secant parallel to itself, away from the graph. At some point \(\xi\) between \(a\) and \(b\) the shifted line will just touch the graph without crossing it: it becomes the tangent at \(\xi\). (One such point is the point of the graph that is farthest from the secant.) Since the tangent is parallel to the secant, the two have the same slope: \[ f'(\xi) = \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}. \]

Exercise 18.1 Try to draw the graph of a function on \([a,b]\) for which there is no point \(\xi\in(a,b)\) at which the tangent is parallel to the secant. Is your function continuous? Does it have a tangent at every point?

18.2 The Mean Value Theorem

Theorem 18.1 (Mean Value Theorem) Suppose \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\) and differentiable on \((a,b)\). Then there exists \(\xi\) with \(a<\xi<b\) and \[ f'(\xi) = \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}. \]

Equivalently, \(f(b)-f(a) = f'(\xi)\,(b-a)\). The theorem asserts only that such a \(\xi\) exists; it says nothing about where in \((a,b)\) it lies, and there may be several such points. Note also that \(f\) must be continuous on the closed interval, but differentiability is only required on the open interval: the derivative need not exist at the endpoints.

Why do we need to assume that \(f'(x)\) exists for all \(x\in(a,b)\)? A single point of non-differentiability is enough to break the theorem.

Example 18.1
Let \(f(x) = \lvert x \rvert\) on \([-1,1]\). Then \(f\) is continuous on \([-1,1]\), and the secant has slope \[ \frac{f(1)-f(-1)}{1-(-1)} = \frac{1-1}{2} = 0 . \] But \(f'(x) = -1\) for \(x<0\) and \(f'(x)=1\) for \(x>0\), while \(f'(0)\) does not exist (the left- and right-sided limits of the difference quotient are \(-1\) and \(1\)). So there is no \(\xi\in(-1,1)\) with \(f'(\xi)=0\). The graph has a corner at \(0\), and no tangent is parallel to the secant.

Example 18.2 (Driving)
Suppose you drive for \(2\) hours and end up \(120\) miles from where you started. Let \(s(t)\) be your distance from the starting point at time \(t\in[0,2]\). Assume that your movement is continuous and that your velocity \(s'(t)\) exists at all times. By Theorem 18.1, there is a moment \(\xi\in(0,2)\) at which your velocity was \[ s'(\xi) = \frac{s(2)-s(0)}{2-0} = \frac{120-0}{2-0} = 60 \text{ mph}. \] Here the numerator is a distance and the denominator a time. In particular, you cannot claim to have driven at less than \(50\) mph the whole time. On the other hand, the theorem does not tell us when you were going exactly \(60\) mph.

18.3 Rolle’s Theorem

We prove the Mean Value Theorem by first establishing the special case of a horizontal secant.

Theorem 18.2 (Rolle’s Theorem) Suppose \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\) and differentiable on \((a,b)\), and \(f(a)=f(b)=0\). Then there exists \(\xi\) with \(a<\xi<b\) and \[ f'(\xi) = 0. \]

This is the Mean Value Theorem with a horizontal tangent: if the graph of \(f\) meets the \(x\)-axis at \(a\) and at \(b\), it must have a horizontal tangent somewhere in between.

The proof rests on the following key fact, which we have used before (see Remark 11.2) and will prove later.

Theorem 18.3 (Extreme Value Theorem) A continuous function on a closed interval \([a,b]\) assumes a global maximum and a global minimum. That is, there exist \(M\) and \(m\) such that \[ m \le f(x) \le M \qquad\text{for all } x\in[a,b], \] and there are points \(x_m, x_M\in[a,b]\) with \(f(x_m)=m\) and \(f(x_M)=M\).

With this, we can prove Theorem 18.2.

NoteProof

Proof. Let \(m\) and \(M\) be the minimum and maximum of \(f\) on \([a,b]\) (Theorem 18.3). Since \(f(a)=f(b)=0\), we must have \[ m \le 0 \le M . \]

Case \(m = M = 0\). Then \(f(x)=0\) for all \(x\in[a,b]\). Every difference quotient of \(f\) is \(0\), so \(f'(\xi) = 0\) for every \(\xi\in(a,b)\), and we are done.

Case \(M>0\). There exists \(\xi\in[a,b]\) with \(f(\xi)=M\). Since \(f(a)=f(b)=0<M\), \(\xi\) is an interior point: \(\xi\in(a,b)\). Since \(f(x)\le M\) for all \(x\in[a,b]\), we have \[ f(\xi+h) - f(\xi) \le 0 \] for all \(h\) with \(\xi+h\in[a,b]\), in particular for all small enough \(\lvert h \rvert\). Hence \[ \frac{f(\xi+h)-f(\xi)}{h} \le 0 \quad\text{for } h>0, \qquad\qquad \frac{f(\xi+h)-f(\xi)}{h} \ge 0 \quad\text{for } h<0 . \] Since \(f\) is differentiable at \(\xi\), the limit \[ \lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(\xi+h)-f(\xi)}{h} \] exists, no matter from which direction \(h\to0\). Letting \(h\to0\) through positive values, the limit is \(\le0\); letting \(h\to0\) through negative values, it is \(\ge0\) (a limit of nonpositive numbers is nonpositive; compare the argument at the end of the proof of Theorem 10.2). Since the left-sided and the right-sided limit agree (Definition 10.2), we must have \[ f'(\xi) = \lim_{h\to0}\frac{f(\xi+h)-f(\xi)}{h} = 0 . \]

Case \(M=0\), \(m<0\). The same argument, applied at a point \(\xi\) where \(f\) attains its minimum \(m\) (or the previous case applied to \(-f\)), gives \(f'(\xi)=0\).

Remark 18.1. The proof shows more than Rolle’s theorem: if \(f\) attains a maximum or a minimum at an interior point \(\xi\) of an interval, and \(f\) is differentiable at \(\xi\), then \(f'(\xi)=0\). The assumption \(f(a)=f(b)=0\) was only used to guarantee that an extremum occurs in the interior.

18.4 Proof of the Mean Value Theorem

To deduce Theorem 18.1 from Theorem 18.2, we subtract the secant from \(f\). Define \[ \varphi(x) := f(x) - f(a) - \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}\,(x-a). \] The secant through \((a,f(a))\) and \((b,f(b))\) is the line \(y = f(a) + \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}(x-a)\), so \(\varphi(x)\) is the vertical distance between the point \((x,f(x))\) on the graph and the point of the secant above (or below) \(x\).

NoteProof

Proof. Plugging in the endpoints, \[ \varphi(a) = f(a) - f(a) - 0 = 0, \qquad \varphi(b) = f(b) - f(a) - \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}(b-a) = 0 . \] The function \(\varphi\) is of the form \[ \varphi(x) = f(x) + c\,x + d, \qquad\text{with constants}\quad c = -\frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}, \quad d = -f(a) + \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}\,a . \] So \(\varphi\) is continuous on \([a,b]\) (Corollary 10.1), and since \[ \frac{\varphi(x+h)-\varphi(x)}{h} = \frac{f(x+h)-f(x)}{h} + c , \] it is differentiable on \((a,b)\) with \[ \varphi'(x) = f'(x) + c . \] By Theorem 18.2 there exists \(\xi\) with \(a<\xi<b\) and \(\varphi'(\xi)=0\). Hence \[ f'(\xi) = -c = \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a} . \]

Remark 18.2. Applying Theorem 18.1 to a function with \(f(a)=f(b)\) gives \(f'(\xi)=0\). So Rolle’s theorem holds more generally whenever \(f(a)=f(b)\), not only when both values are \(0\).

18.5 Application: Lipschitz Continuity

Definition 18.1
A function \(f\) is Lipschitz continuous on an interval \(I\) if there is a constant \(L\ge0\) such that \[ \lvert f(x)-f(y) \rvert \le L\,\lvert x-y \rvert \qquad\text{for all } x,y\in I . \]

In other words, all difference quotients \(\frac{f(x)-f(y)}{x-y}\) of \(f\) on \(I\) are bounded in absolute value by \(L\): the secant slopes of \(f\) cannot become arbitrarily steep. A Lipschitz continuous function is continuous: given \(\varepsilon>0\), the choice \(\delta=\varepsilon/L\) (any \(\delta\) if \(L=0\)) works in Definition 6.2 at every point.

We say \(f\) is continuously differentiable on \([a,b]\) if \(f'\) exists on \([a,b]\) (as a one-sided derivative at the endpoints) and \(f'\) is continuous on \([a,b]\).

Proposition 18.1
Any function \(f\) that is continuously differentiable on \([a,b]\) is Lipschitz continuous on \([a,b]\).

NoteProof

Proof. Since \(f'\) is continuous on \([a,b]\), it is bounded there by Theorem 18.3: there is \(L\) with \[ \lvert f'(x) \rvert \le L \qquad\text{for all } x\in[a,b]. \] Now let \(a\le x<y\le b\). Then \(f\) is continuous on \([x,y]\) and differentiable on \((x,y)\), so by Theorem 18.1 there is \(\xi\in(x,y)\) with \[ \lvert f(y)-f(x) \rvert = \lvert f'(\xi)\,(y-x) \rvert = \lvert f'(\xi) \rvert\,\lvert y-x \rvert \le L\,\lvert y-x \rvert . \]

The proof only used that \(f'\) is bounded on \((a,b)\) (and that \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\)), so this weaker assumption is already enough.

Example 18.3 (Not every continuous function is Lipschitz continuous)
Let \(f(x) = \sqrt[3]{x}\) on \([-1,1]\). This function is continuous. But for \(x\neq0\), \[ \frac{f(x)-f(0)}{x-0} = \frac{x^{1/3}}{x} = x^{-2/3}, \] which is not bounded near \(0\): for \(x = \frac1{n^3}\) it equals \(n^2\). So no constant \(L\) can satisfy \(\lvert f(x)-f(0) \rvert\le L\lvert x-0 \rvert\) for all \(x\in[-1,1]\). This does not contradict Proposition 18.1: \(f\) is not differentiable at \(0\) — the graph has a vertical tangent there, and \(f'(x) = \frac13 x^{-2/3}\) is unbounded near \(0\).

18.6 Summary

  • The Mean Value Theorem (Theorem 18.1): if \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\) and differentiable on \((a,b)\), then \(f'(\xi) = \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}\) for some \(\xi\in(a,b)\). Geometrically, some tangent is parallel to the secant. It turns local information (the derivative) into global information (difference quotients). For example, an average speed of \(60\) mph forces a moment of speed exactly \(60\) mph (Example 18.2).
  • Differentiability at every interior point is needed: \(\lvert x \rvert\) on \([-1,1]\) has a corner and no horizontal tangent (Example 18.1).
  • Rolle’s Theorem (Theorem 18.2) is the special case \(f(a)=f(b)=0\). Its proof uses the Extreme Value Theorem (Theorem 18.3): \(f\) attains a maximum or minimum at an interior point \(\xi\), and there the right-sided difference quotients are \(\le0\) and the left-sided ones \(\ge0\) (or vice versa), so \(f'(\xi)=0\) (Remark 18.1).
  • The Mean Value Theorem follows by applying Rolle’s theorem to \(\varphi(x) = f(x) - \big(\text{secant at } x\big)\), which vanishes at \(a\) and \(b\) and has \(\varphi' = f' - \frac{f(b)-f(a)}{b-a}\).
  • Application: a continuously differentiable function on \([a,b]\) is Lipschitz continuous, \(\lvert f(x)-f(y) \rvert\le L\lvert x-y \rvert\) with \(L\) a bound for \(\lvert f' \rvert\) (Proposition 18.1). Continuous functions need not be Lipschitz: \(\sqrt[3]{x}\) has unbounded difference quotients at \(0\) (Example 18.3).
