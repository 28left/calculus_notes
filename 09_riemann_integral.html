11  The Riemann Integral

11.1 Area of the Circle

We start with a problem from antiquity: what is the area of a circle of radius \(1\)? The classical idea (going back to Archimedes) is to approximate the circle by figures whose area we can compute — regular \(n\)-gons inscribed in the circle.

Split the \(n\)-th polygon (\(n\ge3\)) into \(n\) congruent triangles, each with its apex at the center of the circle, two sides of length \(1\), and apex angle \(\tfrac{2\pi}{n}\). Taking one of the two unit sides as base, the height of the triangle is \(h = \sin\!\big(\tfrac{2\pi}{n}\big)\), so \[ \text{area of one triangle} = \frac12\cdot 1\cdot h = \frac{\sin\!\big(\tfrac{2\pi}{n}\big)}{2}, \] \[ A_n := \text{area of the $n$-gon} = \frac{n}{2}\,\sin\!\Big(\frac{2\pi}{n}\Big). \] As \(n\) grows, the polygons hug the circle more and more tightly, and \(A_n\) should approximate the area of the circle better and better.

Formally, we can say two things about the sequence \((A_n)\):

  • \((A_n)\) is increasing.
  • \((A_n)\) is bounded: every inscribed \(n\)-gon is contained in the square of side length \(2\) circumscribing the circle, so \(A_n\le 4\) for all \(n\).

By the Monotone Convergence Theorem (1), \((A_n)\) converges. Its limit is \(\pi\).

Remark 11.1. Two points are glossed over here. Monotonicity is not obvious from the picture, since the \(n\)-gon is not contained in the \((n+1)\)-gon; it follows from the fact that \(\sin(x)/x\) is decreasing on \((0,\pi]\), writing \(A_n = \pi\cdot\dfrac{\sin(2\pi/n)}{2\pi/n}\). The same rewriting shows that identifying the limit as \(\pi\) amounts to knowing \(\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sin x}{x} = 1\) — a limit we will return to when we study derivatives of the trigonometric functions.

How can we generalize this idea to other areas? Triangles could work, but it is not clear how to set this up methodically for an arbitrary region. For regions under the graph of a function, there is a better choice: rectangles, whose area is as easy to compute as it gets.

11.2 Approximation of Areas by Rectangles

Whatever approximation scheme we use, we want it to have the following properties:

  • It uses a geometric figure whose area is easily computable. Rectangles are as easy as it gets.
  • There is a general method to approximate an area by rectangles.
  • There is a general method to refine any approximation.

Areas under Curves

Let \(f\colon[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}\) be continuous with \(f(x)\ge0\) for all \(x\in[a,b]\). We want to compute \[ A := \text{area between the graph of $f$ and the $x$-axis, between $x=a$ and $x=b$}. \]

The idea is as follows.

  • Partition \([a,b]\) into finitely many segments \[ a = x_0 < x_1 < \cdots < x_n = b. \] The segments \([x_{i-1},x_i]\) will form the bases of rectangles.
  • For each segment \([x_{i-1},x_i]\), pick a point \(x_i^*\in[x_{i-1},x_i]\). The height of the \(i\)-th rectangle is \(f(x_i^*)\).

If \(R_i\) denotes the area of the \(i\)-th rectangle, then \[ R_i = \lvert x_i - x_{i-1} \rvert\cdot f(x_i^*) \qquad (i=1,\ldots,n), \] and \(R_1+\cdots+R_n\) is an approximation to \(A\).

Definition 11.1
A finite set of points \[ P:\quad a = x_0 < x_1 < \cdots < x_n = b \] is called a partition of \([a,b]\). Points \(S = \{x_1^*,\ldots,x_n^*\}\) with \(x_i^*\in[x_{i-1},x_i]\) are called sample points, and \[ \Delta_i = \lvert x_i - x_{i-1} \rvert = x_i - x_{i-1} \qquad (i=1,\ldots,n) \] is the length of the \(i\)-th segment. The Riemann sum for \(f\) on \([a,b]\), given \(P\) and \(S\), is \[ RS(P,S) = f(x_1^*)\,\Delta_1 + f(x_2^*)\,\Delta_2 + \cdots + f(x_n^*)\,\Delta_n = \sum_{i=1}^n f(x_i^*)\,\Delta_i . \]

The symbol \(\sum\) is summation (or \(\Sigma\)-) notation: \(\sum_{i=1}^n c_i\) stands for \(c_1+c_2+\cdots+c_n\). Note that Definition 11.1 makes sense for any function \(f\) on \([a,b]\) — neither continuity nor \(f\ge0\) is needed to form the sum. (If \(f\) takes negative values, the corresponding terms simply count as negative area.)

Refinement. How do we make the approximation better? We refine the partition — add more division points so that the segments get shorter — and choose new sample points. The goal is to find a way to do this that is both methodological and practical. The natural requirement is that the length of every segment tends to \(0\). It is convenient to measure this by the length of the longest segment, \[ \|P\| := \max_{1\le i\le n}\Delta_i, \] called the mesh of \(P\). (Other authors call it the span.)

11.3 Equipartitions and the Definition of the Integral

It turns out the approximation process is remarkably robust — several approaches are possible, and they all give the same answer. The most practical one restricts to a special kind of partition.

Equipartitions. All segments have the same length, so only the number of segments matters. With \(n\) segments, \[ \Delta_i = \frac{b-a}{n} =: \Delta \quad\text{for all } i, \qquad\qquad x_i = a + i\Delta. \] There are three standard ways to choose sample points:

  • left endpoints: \(x_i^* = x_{i-1}\),
  • right endpoints: \(x_i^* = x_i\),
  • midpoints: \(x_i^* = \bar x_i := \dfrac{x_{i-1}+x_i}{2}\).

This gives three sequences of Riemann sums: \[ LHS_n = \sum_{i=1}^n f(x_{i-1})\,\Delta, \qquad RHS_n = \sum_{i=1}^n f(x_i)\,\Delta, \qquad MS_n = \sum_{i=1}^n f(\bar x_i)\,\Delta, \] the left-hand sum, the right-hand sum, and the midpoint sum.

Theorem 11.1
If \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\), then all three limits \[ \lim_{n\to\infty} LHS_n, \qquad \lim_{n\to\infty} RHS_n, \qquad \lim_{n\to\infty} MS_n \] exist and are equal.

Definition 11.2
Let \(f\) be continuous on \([a,b]\). The (Riemann) integral of \(f\) from \(a\) to \(b\) is the common value \(L\) of the limits in Theorem 11.1: \[ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = L = \lim_{n\to\infty} LHS_n = \lim_{n\to\infty} RHS_n = \lim_{n\to\infty} MS_n. \]

The notation is Leibniz’s: the integral sign \(\int\) is an elongated S for “sum”, and \(dx\) recalls the lengths \(\Delta\). It is only notation — \(dx\) on its own has no meaning here. Likewise, the name of the variable is irrelevant: \(\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = \int_a^b f(t)\,dt\).

11.4 Why the Choice Does Not Matter (optional)

Theorem 11.1 is a special case of a much stronger statement: for a continuous function it does not matter at all how we partition or where we sample, as long as the segments get uniformly short.

Theorem 11.2 (Existence of the integral) Let \(f\) be continuous on \([a,b]\). There is a unique number \(I\) with the following property: for every sequence of partitions \(P_1,P_2,\ldots\) of \([a,b]\) with \(\|P_n\|\to0\), and every choice of sample points \(S_n\) for \(P_n\), \[ \lim_{n\to\infty} RS(P_n,S_n) = I. \]

Since an equipartition into \(n\) segments has mesh \(\tfrac{b-a}{n}\to0\), Theorem 11.1 follows at once, with \(\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = I\). We now sketch why Theorem 11.2 is true. The argument is informal in two places, flagged in Remark 11.2 below.

Step 1: Trapping Every Riemann Sum

A continuous function on a closed interval attains a maximum and a minimum there. (This is the Extreme Value Theorem, which we have not proved yet.) So for a partition \(P\) we can put \[ m_i = \min\{f(x) : x_{i-1}\le x\le x_i\}, \qquad M_i = \max\{f(x) : x_{i-1}\le x\le x_i\}, \] and form the lower and upper (Darboux) sums \[ L(P) = \sum_{i=1}^n m_i\,\Delta_i, \qquad\qquad U(P) = \sum_{i=1}^n M_i\,\Delta_i. \] Geometrically (for \(f\ge0\)), \(L(P)\) is the area of the largest rectangles on the segments that fit under the graph, and \(U(P)\) is the area of the smallest rectangles that reach over it.

Whatever sample points \(x_i^*\) we choose, \(m_i\le f(x_i^*)\le M_i\), so \[ L(P) \;\le\; RS(P,S) \;\le\; U(P) \qquad\text{for every choice of sample points } S. \] In other words, \(L(P)\) and \(U(P)\) are the worst cases over all choices of sample points. If we can show that they are close to each other, the choice of sample points becomes irrelevant.

Step 2: Refining Moves the Sums Toward Each Other

Suppose \(P'\) is finer than \(P\), i.e. \(P'\) contains all points of \(P\) and possibly more. Adding a single point \(z\) inside a segment \([x_{i-1},x_i]\) splits it into \([x_{i-1},z]\) and \([z,x_i]\). The minimum of \(f\) over each smaller piece is at least the minimum \(m_i\) over the whole segment, and the maximum over each piece is at most \(M_i\). Adding points one at a time, we get \[ L(P) \le L(P') \qquad\text{and}\qquad U(P') \le U(P). \] So refining can only push the lower sum up and the upper sum down.

This has a useful consequence. Given any two partitions \(P\) and \(Q\), the partition \(P\cup Q\) is finer than both, so \[ L(P) \;\le\; L(P\cup Q) \;\le\; U(P\cup Q) \;\le\; U(Q). \] That is: every lower sum is at most every upper sum, no matter which partitions they come from. All lower sums sit to the left of all upper sums on the number line, and by the completeness of \(\mathbb{R}\) (compare the Nested Interval Axiom) there is at least one number \(I\) in between: \[ L(P) \le I \le U(Q) \qquad\text{for all partitions } P, Q. \]

Step 3: Closing the Gap

The gap between upper and lower sum is \[ U(P) - L(P) = \sum_{i=1}^n (M_i - m_i)\,\Delta_i. \] Here continuity enters. On a very short segment a continuous function cannot vary much. Suppose we know the following: for every \(\varepsilon>0\) there is a \(\delta>0\) such that \[ \lvert x-y \rvert<\delta \quad \Rightarrow \quad\lvert f(x)-f(y) \rvert<\varepsilon\qquad\text{for all } x,y\in[a,b]. \tag{11.1}\] Then, as soon as \(\|P\|<\delta\), any two points in the same segment are within \(\delta\) of each other, so \(M_i - m_i<\varepsilon\) for every \(i\), and \[ U(P) - L(P) < \sum_{i=1}^n \varepsilon\,\Delta_i = \varepsilon\,(b-a). \] Since \(\varepsilon\) was arbitrary, the gap can be made as small as we like by making the mesh small.

Step 4: Conclusion

Now let \(\|P_n\|\to0\) and let \(S_n\) be any sample points. By Steps 1 and 2, both \(RS(P_n,S_n)\) and \(I\) lie in the interval \([L(P_n),U(P_n)]\), so \[ \lvert RS(P_n,S_n) - I \rvert \le U(P_n) - L(P_n). \] By Step 3 the right-hand side tends to \(0\), so \(RS(P_n,S_n)\to I\) (by the comparison test, Proposition 8.2). The number \(I\) is unique: any other \(I'\) trapped between all lower and all upper sums satisfies \(\lvert I-I' \rvert\le U(P_n)-L(P_n)\to0\) as well. Nothing in this argument depended on which partitions or sample points we used — only on the mesh going to \(0\).

Remark 11.2. The sketch uses three facts we have not yet established:

  1. the Extreme Value Theorem (continuous functions attain a max and min on closed intervals), used to define \(m_i\) and \(M_i\);
  2. the existence of a number \(I\) between all lower and all upper sums, which is a consequence of the completeness of \(\mathbb{R}\);
  3. condition Equation 11.1, called uniform continuity.

The third point is the subtle one. It looks like Definition 6.2, but the quantifiers are in a different order: in Definition 6.2, \(\delta\) is chosen after the point \(a\), so it may depend on \(a\); in Equation 11.1, one \(\delta\) must work simultaneously for all points of \([a,b]\). For instance, \(f(x)=\tfrac1x\) is continuous on \((0,1]\), but near \(0\) it becomes steeper and steeper, and no single \(\delta\) works for a given \(\varepsilon\). On a closed, bounded interval this cannot happen: a continuous \(f\colon[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}\) is automatically uniformly continuous. Like the Extreme Value Theorem, this can be proved with a bisection / nested interval argument in the spirit of the proof of Theorem 10.2.

Remark 11.3. Continuity really matters. For the Dirichlet function of Example 10.3 on \([0,1]\), every segment of every partition contains both rational and irrational points. Choosing all sample points rational gives \(RS(P,S) = \sum_i 1\cdot\Delta_i = 1\); choosing them all irrational gives \(RS(P,S)=0\) — for every partition, however fine. So the Riemann sums have no common limit, and the Dirichlet function is not Riemann integrable.

11.5 Verifying the Riemann Integral

Before computing anything new, let us check that Definition 11.2 gives the right answer for areas we already know from geometry. By Theorem 11.1 we may use whichever of the three sums is most convenient. Throughout, \(\Delta = \frac{b-a}{n}\) and \(x_i = a + i\Delta\).

Example 11.1
Let \(f(x) = c\). Every sample point gives height \(c\), so \[ LHS_n = RHS_n = \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{b-a}{n}\cdot c = n\cdot\frac{b-a}{n}\cdot c = (b-a)\,c, \] and hence \[ \int_a^b c\,dx = \lim_{n\to\infty} LHS_n = \lim_{n\to\infty}(b-a)\,c = (b-a)\,c, \] the area of a rectangle with base \(b-a\) and height \(c\). ✓

Example 11.2
Let \(f(x) = x\). Using right endpoints \(x_i = a+\frac{b-a}{n}\,i\), \[ \begin{aligned} RHS_n &= \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{b-a}{n}\cdot f\Big(a+\frac{b-a}{n}\,i\Big) = \frac{b-a}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \Big(a+\frac{b-a}{n}\,i\Big) \\ &= \frac{b-a}{n}\Big[\Big(a+\frac{b-a}{n}\cdot1\Big)+\Big(a+\frac{b-a}{n}\cdot2\Big) +\cdots+\Big(a+\frac{b-a}{n}\cdot n\Big)\Big] \\ &= \frac{b-a}{n}\Big(n\,a + \frac{b-a}{n}\cdot\frac{n(n+1)}{2}\Big) \\ &= (b-a)\,a + \frac{b-a}{n}\cdot\frac{b-a}{n}\cdot\frac{n(n+1)}{2} \\ &= (b-a)\,a + \frac{(b-a)^2}{2}\cdot\frac{n+1}{n} \;\xrightarrow{\;n\to\infty\;}\; (b-a)\,a + \frac{(b-a)^2}{2}. \end{aligned} \] Here we used \(1+2+\cdots+n = \frac{n(n+1)}{2}\) and \(\frac{n+1}{n}\to1\).

For \(0\le a<b\) this is exactly what geometry predicts: the region under \(y=x\) between \(a\) and \(b\) is a rectangle of base \(b-a\) and height \(a\) (area \((b-a)\,a\)), topped by a right triangle with both legs of length \(b-a\) (area \(\frac{(b-a)^2}{2}\)). ✓ Simplifying, \[ \int_a^b x\,dx = (b-a)\Big(a + \frac{b-a}{2}\Big) = \frac{(b-a)(b+a)}{2} = \frac{b^2-a^2}{2}. \]

Exercise 11.1 Verify this for an arbitrary linear function \(f(x) = mx + c\): compute \(\lim_{n\to\infty}RHS_n\) and check that the result agrees with the area of the trapezoid under the graph (when \(f\ge0\) on \([a,b]\)).

11.6 “New” Functions: \(x^2\)

The examples above only reproduced areas we already knew. The first genuinely new area is the one under a parabola. Let \(f(x) = x^2\). Then \[ RHS_n = \frac{b-a}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n f\Big(a+\frac{b-a}{n}\,i\Big) = \frac{b-a}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n\Big(a+\frac{b-a}{n}\,i\Big)^2. \]

To make life a little easier, take \(a=0\): \[ \begin{aligned} RHS_n &= \frac{b}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n\Big(\frac{b}{n}\,i\Big)^2 = \frac{b}{n}\cdot\frac{b^2}{n^2}\,\big(1^2+2^2+\cdots+n^2\big) \\ &= \frac{b^3}{n^3}\cdot\frac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{6} = \frac{b^3}{6}\Big(1+\frac1n\Big)\Big(2+\frac1n\Big) \;\xrightarrow{\;n\to\infty\;}\; \frac{b^3\cdot 2}{6} = \frac{b^3}{3}. \end{aligned} \] So \[ \int_0^b x^2\,dx = \frac{b^3}{3}. \]

Exercise 11.2 Prove by induction that \(1^2+2^2+\cdots+n^2 = \dfrac{n(n+1)(2n+1)}{6}\) for all \(n\in\mathbb{N}\).

Exercise 11.3 Generalize the computation above to show that for arbitrary \(a<b\), \[ \int_a^b x^2\,dx = \frac{b^3-a^3}{3}. \] (Expand \(\big(a+\frac{b-a}{n}\,i\big)^2\) and use the formulas for \(\sum i\) and \(\sum i^2\).)

11.7 Summary

  • The area of the unit circle is the limit of the areas \(A_n = \frac n2\sin\!\big(\frac{2\pi}{n}\big)\) of inscribed regular \(n\)-gons; the sequence is increasing and bounded, so it converges by
  • For areas under a graph we use rectangles: a partition \(P\) of \([a,b]\) and sample points \(S\) give the Riemann sum \(RS(P,S) = \sum_{i=1}^n f(x_i^*)\,\Delta_i\) (Definition 11.1). We improve the approximation by refining the partition so that the mesh \(\|P\|=\max_i\Delta_i\) tends to \(0\).
  • Restricting to equipartitions with left endpoints, right endpoints, or midpoints gives three sequences \(LHS_n\), \(RHS_n\), \(MS_n\). For continuous \(f\) they have a common limit (Theorem 11.1), which defines \(\int_a^b f(x)\,dx\) (Definition 11.2).
  • More generally, for continuous \(f\) every sequence of Riemann sums with mesh \(\to0\) converges to the same number (Theorem 11.2). The idea: every Riemann sum is trapped between the lower and upper sums \(L(P)\le RS(P,S)\le U(P)\); refining pushes these toward each other; and uniform continuity makes the gap \(U(P)-L(P)\) smaller than \(\varepsilon(b-a)\) once the mesh is small (Remark 11.2). Without continuity this can fail, as the Dirichlet function shows (Remark 11.3).
  • The definition reproduces known areas — \(\int_a^b c\,dx = (b-a)c\) (Example 11.1) and \(\int_a^b x\,dx = \frac{b^2-a^2}{2}\) (Example 11.2) — and computes new ones: \(\int_0^b x^2\,dx = \frac{b^3}{3}\).
