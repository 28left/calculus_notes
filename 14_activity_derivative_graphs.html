17  Activity: Reading Derivatives from Graphs

17.1 Background

For now we use the geometric meaning of the derivative: \(f'(x_0)\) is the slope of the tangent line to the graph of \(f\) at the point \((x_0, f(x_0))\). Reading off a slope from a graph means laying a ruler along the curve at \(x_0\) and measuring rise over run, using the grid squares. Doing this at every \(x\) produces a new function, \(x \mapsto f'(x)\), with its own graph.

17.2 Warm-up: a dictionary

Exercise 17.1 Complete the right-hand column. Each row translates a feature of the graph of \(f\) into a feature of the graph of \(f'\).

If the graph of \(f\) … … then the graph of \(f'\) …
is increasing on an interval  
is decreasing on an interval  
has a horizontal tangent at \(x_0\)  
is a straight line of slope \(m\) on an interval  
bends upward (is concave up) on an interval  
bends downward (is concave down) on an interval  
changes from bending down to bending up (inflection point) at \(x_0\)  
has a corner at \(x_0\)  
NoteSolution

Solution.

If the graph of \(f\) … … then the graph of \(f'\) …
is increasing on an interval lies on or above the \(x\)-axis there
is decreasing on an interval lies on or below the \(x\)-axis there
has a horizontal tangent at \(x_0\) meets the \(x\)-axis at \(x_0\): \(f'(x_0)=0\)
is a straight line of slope \(m\) on an interval is the horizontal line at height \(m\) there
bends upward (is concave up) on an interval is increasing there
bends downward (is concave down) on an interval is decreasing there
changes from bending down to bending up at \(x_0\) has a local minimum at \(x_0\)
has a corner at \(x_0\) has no value at \(x_0\); it typically jumps there

Note “on or above” in the first row, not “above”: \(x \mapsto x^3\) is strictly increasing, yet its tangent at \(0\) is horizontal. A zero of \(f'\) does not by itself signal a maximum or minimum. Problems 2 and 4 contain exactly this situation.

17.3 Part I: From \(f\) to \(f'\)

In each problem, the top panel shows the graph of \(f\). Sketch the graph of \(f'\) on the empty axes below it; the \(x\)-axes are aligned, so you can transfer \(x\)-coordinates straight down.

Problem 1

Exercise 17.2  

  1. Find all points where the tangent to \(f\) is horizontal. Mark the corresponding zeros of \(f'\) on the lower axes.

  2. Between these points, decide whether \(f\) is increasing or decreasing, and hence whether the graph of \(f'\) lies above or below the \(x\)-axis.

  3. Where is \(f\) steepest while going down? What does that tell you about \(f'\) there?

  4. Estimate slopes with a ruler and fill in the table. Plot the points on the lower axes.

    \(x\) \(-4\) \(-3\) \(-2\) \(-1\) \(0\) \(1\) \(2\) \(3\) \(4\)
    \(f'(x)\approx\)                  

  5. Connect your points to a smooth sketch of \(f'\). Check it against the dictionary: where \(f\) bends up, is your \(f'\) increasing?

NoteSolution

Solution. The function is \(f(x) = \tfrac18(x^3 - 12x)\), so \(f'(x) = \tfrac38(x^2-4)\), an upward parabola.

\(x\) \(-4\) \(-3\) \(-2\) \(-1\) \(0\) \(1\) \(2\) \(3\) \(4\)
\(f'(x)\) \(4.5\) \(1.875\) \(0\) \(-1.125\) \(-1.5\) \(-1.125\) \(0\) \(1.875\) \(4.5\)
  • Horizontal tangents at \(x=\pm2\) (local max at \(-2\), local min at \(2\)), so \(f'(\pm2)=0\).
  • \(f\) increases on \([-4,-2]\) and \([2,4]\) (\(f'\ge 0\)) and decreases on \([-2,2]\) (\(f'\le 0\)).
  • The steepest descent is at the inflection point \(x=0\), where \(f'\) has its minimum \(-1.5\).
  • \(f\) bends down on \([-4,0]\) and up on \([0,4]\), matching \(f'\) decreasing, then increasing.

Problem 2

Exercise 17.3  

  1. At \(x=-1\) the tangent to \(f\) is horizontal. Does \(f\) have a maximum or a minimum there? What does the graph of \(f'\) do near \(x=-1\) — does it cross the \(x\)-axis or only touch it?
  2. To the right of \(x=1\) the graph of \(f\) is a straight line. What is its slope, and what does \(f'\) look like there?
  3. What happens at \(x = 1\)? Compare the slopes just to the left and just to the right of \(1\). Is there a sensible value for \(f'(1)\)? How do you show this in your sketch?
  4. Sketch \(f'\) on the whole interval \([-3,3]\).
NoteSolution

Solution. On \([-3,1]\), \(f(x) = \tfrac14(x+1)^3 + 1\), so \(f'(x) = \tfrac34(x+1)^2\); on \([1,3]\), \(f(x) = 4-x\), so \(f'(x) = -1\).

  • At \(x=-1\) the tangent is horizontal, yet \(f\) keeps increasing on both sides: there is no extremum. Accordingly the graph of \(f'\) touches the \(x\)-axis at \(-1\) without crossing it. (A “flat” inflection point, as for \(x^3\) at \(0\).)
  • On \((1,3]\) the graph is a line of slope \(-1\), so \(f'\) is constantly \(-1\).
  • Just left of \(1\) the slope is close to \(3\); just right of \(1\) it is \(-1\). No single tangent line fits at the corner, so \(f'(1)\) is undefined, and the graph of \(f'\) jumps from \(3\) to \(-1\). We draw open circles at both ends. Note that \(f\) itself is continuous at \(1\): continuity does not guarantee a derivative (C&J §2.8.e).

17.4 Part II: From \(f'\) back to \(f\)

Now the roles are reversed: the top panel shows the graph of the derivative \(f'\), and your task is to sketch \(f\) itself.

Problem 3

Exercise 17.4  

  1. Read off from the graph of \(f'\): on which intervals is \(f\) increasing or decreasing? Where does \(f\) have a local maximum or minimum? Where does \(f\) bend up, where does it bend down, and where is it a straight line (with what slope)?
  2. Sketch a function \(f\) with this derivative on axes A.
  3. Compare your sketch with your neighbor’s. Are they the same? On axes A, sketch a second function that also has the derivative shown, but is different from your first one. How are the two graphs related to each other?
  4. Describe all functions whose derivative is the one shown. How much information about \(f\) is the graph of \(f'\) missing?
  5. Now suppose you are also told that \[ f(-2) = -1. \] Sketch \(f\) on axes B. Is there now only one possible graph? Read off approximate values of \(f(-4)\), \(f(0)\), \(f(2)\), \(f(3)\), and \(f(4)\) from your sketch.
NoteSolution

Solution.

  1. \(f' < 0\) on \([-4,-2)\) and \((3,4]\), \(f'>0\) on \((-2,3)\): \(f\) decreases, then increases, then decreases, with a local minimum at \(x=-2\) and a local maximum at \(x=3\). Since \(f'\) increases on \([-4,0]\), \(f\) bends up there; on \([0,2]\), \(f'=2\) is constant, so \(f\) is a straight line of slope \(2\); on \([2,4]\), \(f'\) decreases, so \(f\) bends down.
  2. A correct sketch has all the features from part 1; where it sits vertically is up to you.
  3. Any two correct sketches differ by a vertical shift: the derivative only records slopes, and sliding a graph up or down changes no slope.
  4. If \(f\) is one such function, the others are exactly the functions \(f + C\), \(C\) a constant. That every function with the same derivative arises this way is a real theorem, not just a picture: it says that a function with derivative \(0\) on an interval is constant, which follows from the mean value theorem (C&J §2.8.h, §2.9.b). The graph of \(f'\) determines \(f\) only up to this one unknown number.
  5. The condition \(f(-2) = -1\) pins down the constant: of all the vertical shifts, exactly one passes through the point \((-2,-1)\). The red graph is the unique answer, with \(f(-4)=1\), \(f(0)=1\), \(f(2)=5\), \(f(3)=6\), \(f(4)=5\). (Explicitly: \(f(x)=\tfrac12x^2+2x+1\) on \([-4,0]\), \(f(x)=2x+1\) on \([0,2]\), \(f(x)=-x^2+6x-3\) on \([2,4]\).)

Problem 4

Here \(f\) is continuous on \([-3,3]\) and differentiable everywhere except at \(x=0\); its derivative is shown below.

Exercise 17.5  

  1. Read off the main features of \(f\). In particular: what kind of point is \(x=-1\)? What does the graph of \(f\) look like at \(x=0\)? Does \(f\) have a maximum or minimum at \(x=2\)?
  2. On axes A, sketch two different functions with the derivative shown.
  3. Now you are told that \[ f(2) = 1. \] Sketch \(f\) on axes B. Read off approximate values of \(f(-3)\), \(f(-1)\), \(f(0)\), and \(f(3)\).
NoteSolution

Solution.

  1. \(f'\) changes sign from \(-\) to \(+\) at \(x=-1\): a local minimum. At \(x=0\) the slope jumps from \(1\) to \(3\): \(f\) has a corner there (the reverse of Problem 2). At \(x=2\), \(f'\) touches zero without changing sign: \(f\) has a horizontal tangent but keeps increasing — no extremum. \(f\) bends up on \([-3,0]\) and on \([2,3]\), and bends down on \([0,2]\).
  2. Again, any two answers differ by a vertical shift.
  3. Only one shift passes through \((2,1)\): \(f(-3)=0.5\), \(f(-1)=-1.5\), \(f(0)=-1\), \(f(3)=1.25\). To reach the left half from the given point you must pass through the corner, and this is where the continuity of \(f\) at \(0\) is used (see Going Further, question 3).

17.5 Going further

Exercise 17.6  

  1. Change as area. In Problem 3, compute the signed area between the graph of \(f'\) and the \(x\)-axis from \(x=-2\) to \(x=3\) (it is made of triangles and a rectangle). Compare it with \(f(3) - f(-2)\) from your sketch. Repeat for another pair of points. What do you conjecture?
  2. Why the real numbers matter. Define \(h\) on the rational numbers by \(h(x) = 0\) if \(x < \sqrt2\) and \(h(x) = 1\) if \(x > \sqrt2\). Convince yourself that at every rational \(x\), the graph of \(h\) is flat nearby, so its “derivative” is \(0\) everywhere on \(\mathbb{Q}\). Yet \(h\) is not constant. Which property of \(\mathbb{R}\) (from the first weeks of the course) rules this out for functions on a real interval?
  3. Is one value always enough? In Problem 4, drop the assumption that \(f\) is continuous at \(0\), so \(f\) is only required to have the given derivative on \([-3,0)\) and on \((0,3]\). Does \(f(2) = 1\) still determine \(f\)? How many function values would you need?
NoteSolution

Solution.

  1. The area is \(2\) (triangle on \([-2,0]\)) \(+\ 4\) (rectangle on \([0,2]\)) \(+\ 1\) (triangle on \([2,3]\)) \(= 7\), and indeed \(f(3) - f(-2) = 6 - (-1) = 7\). The conjecture \[ f(b) - f(a) = \int_a^b f'(x)\,dx \] is the fundamental theorem of calculus (C&J §2.9). It explains the ambiguity in a second way: \(f'\) determines every difference \(f(b)-f(a)\), but not the starting height.
  2. On \(\mathbb{Q}\) the jump of \(h\) happens across a “hole” at \(\sqrt2\) that contains no input of \(h\). On \(\mathbb{R}\) there are no such holes: this is completeness (the nested interval principle). The proof that \(f'=0\) forces \(f\) constant goes through the mean value theorem, which in turn rests on completeness.
  3. No. On each of the two pieces \(f\) is determined up to its own constant, and without continuity the two constants are independent: the left half of the graph can be shifted up or down on its own. You need one value from each side of \(0\) — for instance \(f(2)\) and \(f(-1)\). The principle is: one value per interval on which \(f\) is differentiable.
