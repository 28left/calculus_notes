10  Limits of Functions

NoteCompanion reading

This lecture corresponds to C&J §1.8 (the concept of limit for a function of a continuous variable), tying together §1.2.d (continuity) with the Intermediate Value Theorem of §1.2.e.

10.1 From Sequences to Functions

We already know what it means for a sequence to converge (Definition 8.2). We now transfer this idea to functions: instead of asking what happens to \(a_n\) as \(n\to\infty\), we ask what happens to \(f(x)\) as \(x\) approaches some fixed point \(a\). The cheapest way to make this precise is to reduce it to something we already understand — sequences — by feeding every sequence approaching \(a\) into \(f\) and demanding that the outputs all converge to the same value.

Definition 10.1
Let \(f\colon D\to\mathbb{R}\) with \(D\subseteq\mathbb{R}\), and let \(a\in\mathbb{R}\). We say \[ \lim_{x\to a} f(x) = L \] if for every sequence \((x_n)\) with \(x_n\in D\), \(x_n\neq a\), and \(x_n\to a\), we have \(f(x_n)\to L\).

Remark 10.1. The word “every” is doing all the work: it is not enough to find some sequence \(x_n\to a\) with \(f(x_n)\to L\) — the same \(L\) must come out no matter which approaching sequence we feed in. This mirrors uniqueness of sequential limits (Remark 8.1): if two different sequences \(x_n\to a\) and \(x_n'\to a\) produced different outputs \(f(x_n)\to L\) and \(f(x_n')\to L'\neq L\), the limit \(\lim_{x\to a}f(x)\) would simply not exist.

Example 10.1
Let \(f(x) = x^2\). Then \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0} f(x) = 0\).

NoteProof

Proof. Let \((x_n)\) be an arbitrary sequence with \(x_n\to 0\). We may assume \(\lvert x_n \rvert\le 1\) for all \(n\) (true eventually, since \(x_n\to0\)). Then \[ 0 \le f(x_n) = x_n^2 \le \lvert x_n \rvert, \] and \(\lvert x_n \rvert\to 0\), so by the comparison test (Proposition 8.2), \(x_n^2 \to 0\). Since \((x_n)\) was an arbitrary sequence converging to \(0\), Definition 10.1 gives \(\lim_{x\to0} f(x) = 0\).

10.2 The \(\varepsilon\)–\(\delta\) Version

Just as with continuity (Definition 6.2), the sequential definition of a function limit has an equivalent \(\varepsilon\)–\(\delta\) formulation.

Proposition 10.1 (\(\varepsilon\)–\(\delta\) characterization of a limit) Let \(f\colon D\to\mathbb{R}\), \(a\in\mathbb{R}\), \(L\in\mathbb{R}\). Then \[ \lim_{x\to a} f(x) = L \] if and only if \[ \text{for every } \varepsilon>0 \text{ there exists } \delta>0 \text{ s.t. } x\in D,\ 0<\lvert x-a \rvert<\delta \quad \Rightarrow \quad\lvert f(x)-L \rvert<\varepsilon. \]

The proof is the same idea as the equivalence used implicitly for continuity: if the \(\varepsilon\)–\(\delta\) condition holds and \(x_n\to a\), choosing \(N\) so that \(\lvert x_n-a \rvert<\delta\) for \(n\ge N\) forces \(\lvert f(x_n)-L \rvert<\varepsilon\) for \(n\ge N\), i.e. \(f(x_n)\to L\). Conversely, if the \(\varepsilon\)–\(\delta\) condition fails for some \(\varepsilon>0\), then for every \(\delta = \tfrac1n\) we can pick a point \(x_n\) with \(0<\lvert x_n-a \rvert<\tfrac1n\) but \(\lvert f(x_n)-L \rvert\ge\varepsilon\) — producing a sequence \(x_n\to a\) with \(f(x_n)\not\to L\), so the sequential definition fails too.

The only difference from Definition 6.2 is the punctured condition \(0<\lvert x-a \rvert\): a limit does not care what happens (or fails to happen) at \(a\) itself, only nearby. This is what lets us connect the two notions.

Proposition 10.2 (Continuity, restated) Let \(f\colon D\to\mathbb{R}\) and \(a\in D\). Then \(f\) is continuous at \(a\) (Definition 6.2) if and only if \[ \lim_{x\to a} f(x) \text{ exists and equals } f(a). \]

This is exactly Definition 6.2 with the roles of \(x\) and \(a\) relabeled: continuity says \(\lvert x-a \rvert<\delta \quad \Rightarrow \quad\lvert f(x)-f(a) \rvert<\varepsilon\), which is the \(\varepsilon\)–\(\delta\) condition of Proposition 10.1 with \(L=f(a)\) (the point \(x=a\) itself trivially satisfies \(\lvert f(x)-f(a) \rvert=0<\varepsilon\), so punctured and unpunctured versions agree once \(L=f(a)\)).

10.3 Examples

Example 10.2
\(f(x) = x^3\) is continuous at every \(a\in\mathbb{R}\).

NoteProof

Proof. Let \(x_n\to a\). By the limit laws for sequences (Proposition 8.1), \[ x_n^3 = x_n\cdot x_n\cdot x_n \longrightarrow a\cdot a\cdot a = a^3. \] Hence \(f(x_n)\to f(a)\) for every sequence \(x_n\to a\), so \(\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = f(a)\), and Proposition 10.2 gives continuity at \(a\).

Example 10.3
The Dirichlet function \[ f(x) = \begin{cases} 1 & x\in\mathbb{Q}\\ 0 & x\notin\mathbb{Q}\end{cases} \] is discontinuous at every \(a\in\mathbb{R}\).

Take \(a=1\). Consider the two sequences \[ x_n = 1+\frac1n \quad(\text{rational}), \qquad\qquad y_n = 1+\frac{\sqrt2}{n} \quad(\text{irrational}). \] Both converge to \(1\), but \[ f(x_n) \equiv 1 \to 1, \qquad\qquad f(y_n) \equiv 0 \to 0 \neq f(1) = 1. \] Since two sequences converging to \(a=1\) produce different limits for \(f\), \(\lim_{x\to1}f(x)\) does not exist (Remark 10.1), so \(f\) is not continuous at \(1\).

Exercise 10.1 Adapt the argument of Example 10.3 to an arbitrary \(a\in\mathbb{R}\): exhibit a rational sequence and an irrational sequence, both converging to \(a\), on which the Dirichlet function takes different limiting values. Conclude that \(f\) is discontinuous at every \(a\in\mathbb{R}\).

Example 10.4
Let \[ f(x) = \begin{cases} x^3+16 & x<0 \\ -2x+3 & x\ge 0. \end{cases} \] Take \(x_n = -\tfrac1n \to 0\). Then \[ f(x_n) = \left(-\frac1n\right)^{\!3} + 16 \longrightarrow 16 \neq f(0) = 3, \] so \(f\) is discontinuous at \(0\). We will see below exactly what kind of discontinuity this is.

10.4 One-Sided Limits

For Example 10.4, the failure of continuity comes from the two “branches” of \(f\) disagreeing at \(0\) — approaching from the left gives a different answer than approaching from the right. To capture this we restrict which sequences \(x_n\to a\) we are allowed to test with.

Definition 10.2
Let \(f\colon D\to\mathbb{R}\) and \(a\in\mathbb{R}\).

  • \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to a^-} f(x) = L\) (the left-sided limit) if \(f(x_n)\to L\) for every increasing sequence \(x_n \nearrow a\) with \(x_n\in D\).
  • \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to a^+} f(x) = L\) (the right-sided limit) if \(f(x_n)\to L\) for every decreasing sequence \(x_n \searrow a\) with \(x_n\in D\).

Remark 10.2. The left- and right-sided limits may disagree. For \(f\) as in Example 10.4, \[ \lim_{x\to0^-} f(x) = 16, \qquad\qquad \lim_{x\to0^+} f(x) = 3, \] since \(x^3+16\to16\) along any \(x_n\nearrow0\), while \(-2x+3\to3\) along any \(x_n\searrow0\).

Definition 10.3
\(f\) has a jump discontinuity at \(a\) if both one-sided limits exist and are finite, but \[ \lim_{x\to a^-} f(x) \neq \lim_{x\to a^+} f(x). \] This makes precise the informal description from Remark 6.3, and excludes the unbounded case of Remark 6.4, where no finite one-sided limit exists at all. The function of Example 10.4 has a jump discontinuity at \(0\).

Theorem 10.1
Let \(f\colon D\to\mathbb{R}\) and \(a\in D\). Then \(f\) is continuous at \(a\) if and only if \[ \lim_{x\to a^-} f(x) = f(a) = \lim_{x\to a^+} f(x). \]

Like the two-sided limit (Proposition 10.1), each one-sided limit has an equivalent \(\varepsilon\)–\(\delta\) form obtained by restricting to one side: \[ \lim_{x\to a^-} f(x) = L \quad \Leftrightarrow \quad\forall\varepsilon>0\ \exists\delta>0:\ a-\delta<x<a \quad \Rightarrow \quad\lvert f(x)-L \rvert<\varepsilon, \] and symmetrically for \(\lim_{x\to a^+}f(x)=L\) with \(a<x<a+\delta\).

NoteProof

Proof. If \(f\) is continuous at \(a\), then \(f(x_n)\to f(a)\) for every sequence \(x_n\to a\) (Proposition 10.2) — in particular for every increasing sequence \(x_n\nearrow a\) and every decreasing sequence \(x_n\searrow a\), giving both one-sided limits equal to \(f(a)\).

Conversely, suppose both one-sided limits equal \(f(a)\), and let \(\varepsilon>0\). By the \(\varepsilon\)–\(\delta\) forms above there exist \(\delta_1,\delta_2>0\) with \[ a-\delta_1<x<a \quad \Rightarrow \quad\lvert f(x)-f(a) \rvert<\varepsilon, \qquad\qquad a<x<a+\delta_2 \quad \Rightarrow \quad\lvert f(x)-f(a) \rvert<\varepsilon. \] Let \(\delta=\min(\delta_1,\delta_2)\). If \(0<\lvert x-a \rvert<\delta\), then either \(a-\delta<x<a\) or \(a<x<a+\delta\), so in both cases \(\lvert f(x)-f(a) \rvert<\varepsilon\); and \(x=a\) trivially gives \(\lvert f(x)-f(a) \rvert=0<\varepsilon\). So \(\lvert x-a \rvert<\delta \quad \Rightarrow \quad\lvert f(x)-f(a) \rvert<\varepsilon\), which is exactly Definition 6.2.

10.5 Limit Laws for Functions

Because Definition 10.1 reduces everything to sequences, the arithmetic of function limits carries over directly from the arithmetic of sequence limits (Proposition 8.1).

Proposition 10.3 (Arithmetic of function limits) Suppose \(\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = L\) and \(\lim_{x\to a} g(x) = M\). Then \[ \lim_{x\to a} \big(f(x)+g(x)\big) = L+M, \qquad\qquad \lim_{x\to a} f(x)g(x) = LM, \] and, if \(M\neq0\), \[ \lim_{x\to a} \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \frac{L}{M}. \]

Corollary 10.1 If \(a\in\operatorname{dom}(f)\cap\operatorname{dom}(g)\) and \(f,g\) are continuous at \(a\), then \(f+g\), \(f\cdot g\), and (provided \(g(a)\neq0\)) \(f/g\) are continuous at \(a\).

Example 10.5
\[ \lim_{x\to 0} \frac{x^3-3x+4}{x^2+\cos(x)} = \frac{\lim_{x\to0}x^3 - \lim_{x\to0}3x + \lim_{x\to0}4}{\lim_{x\to0}x^2 + \lim_{x\to0}\cos(x)} = \frac{0-0+4}{0+1} = 4. \]

Example 10.6
Careful: the quotient rule requires the denominator’s limit to be nonzero — it says nothing when both numerator and denominator vanish. For \[ \lim_{x\to a} \frac{x^2-a^2}{x-a}, \] we have \(\lim_{x\to a}(x-a) = 0\), so Proposition 10.3 simply does not apply. Instead, factor first: \[ \frac{x^2-a^2}{x-a} = \frac{(x-a)(x+a)}{x-a} = x+a \qquad (x\neq a), \] and only then take the limit: \(\lim_{x\to a}(x+a) = 2a\).

10.6 Continuity of Combinations and Compositions

Proposition 10.2 lets us move freely between “\(f\) is continuous at \(a\)” and “\(\lim_{x\to a}f(x)=f(a)\)”. The next fact is the one we have already been using informally whenever we wrote something like \(\lim_{x\to a}\cos(h(x)) = \cos\!\big(\lim_{x\to a}h(x)\big)\): a limit may be passed from the outside of a continuous function to its inside.

Proposition 10.4 (Limits pass through continuous functions) Suppose \(\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = L\), and \(g\) is continuous at \(L\). Then \[ \lim_{x\to a} g(f(x)) = g(L) = g\Big(\lim_{x\to a} f(x)\Big). \]

NoteProof

Proof. Let \((x_n)\) be an arbitrary sequence with \(x_n\in\operatorname{dom}(f)\), \(x_n\neq a\), \(x_n\to a\). By hypothesis (Definition 10.1), \(f(x_n)\to L\). Since \(g\) is continuous at \(L\), feeding the sequence \(\big(f(x_n)\big)\to L\) into Definition 6.2 gives \(g(f(x_n))\to g(L)\). As \((x_n)\) was an arbitrary sequence approaching \(a\), Definition 10.1 gives \(\lim_{x\to a}g(f(x)) = g(L)\).

Taking \(L=f(a)\) recovers continuity of a composition:

Corollary 10.2 (Continuity of compositions) If \(f\) is continuous at \(a\) and \(g\) is continuous at \(f(a)\), then \(g\circ f\) is continuous at \(a\).

NoteProof

Proof. Since \(f\) is continuous at \(a\), Proposition 10.2 gives \(\lim_{x\to a}f(x) = f(a)\). Applying Proposition 10.4 with \(L=f(a)\), \[ \lim_{x\to a} (g\circ f)(x) = \lim_{x\to a} g(f(x)) = g(f(a)) = (g\circ f)(a), \] so \(g\circ f\) is continuous at \(a\) by Proposition 10.2.

Together with Corollary 10.1 — continuity of \(f+g\), \(f\cdot g\), and \(f/g\) (where \(g\neq0\)) from continuity of \(f\) and \(g\) — Corollary 10.2 means that any function built out of continuous building blocks by finitely many arithmetic operations and compositions is again continuous wherever it is defined. This is exactly the justification behind phrases like “combination of continuous functions” or “composition of continuous functions” used to evaluate a limit by plugging in, as in Example 10.5 above.

Remark 10.3. The elementary functions — polynomials, rational functions, power and algebraic functions, exponentials, logarithms, and the trigonometric functions — are continuous at every point of their domain. Each basic type can be checked directly from Definition 6.2, the way we checked \(x^3\) in Example 10.2; Corollary 10.1 and Corollary 10.2 then extend continuity to every function assembled from these by arithmetic and composition, such as \(\sqrt{2+\sqrt{2x}}\) or \(\cos\!\big(\pi\sin(x)/x\big)\). Once we develop differentiation, this will also follow a second way: every elementary function is differentiable wherever it is defined, and differentiability will turn out to imply continuity.

10.7 The Intermediate Value Theorem

Theorem 10.2 (Intermediate Value Theorem) Let \(f\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\) be continuous, and suppose \(a<b\) with \(f(a)<0\) and \(f(b)>0\). Then there exists \(c\in(a,b)\) with \(f(c)=0\).

NoteProof

Proof. We construct \(c\) by bisection. Set \(a_1=a\), \(b_1=b\), so \(f(a_1)\le0\le f(b_1)\). Given \(a_n,b_n\) with \(f(a_n)\le0\) and \(f(b_n)\ge0\), let \(m_n = \tfrac{a_n+b_n}{2}\).

  • If \(f(m_n)=0\), we are done — take \(c=m_n\).
  • If \(f(m_n)<0\), set \(a_{n+1}=m_n\), \(b_{n+1}=b_n\).
  • If \(f(m_n)>0\), set \(a_{n+1}=a_n\), \(b_{n+1}=m_n\).

In either of the last two cases \(f(a_{n+1})\le0\le f(b_{n+1})\), so the invariant is preserved, and we continue. If the process never terminates, this produces sequences with \((a_n)\) increasing, \((b_n)\) decreasing, \(a_n\le b_n\) for all \(n\), and \[ b_n - a_n = \frac{b-a}{2^{n-1}} \longrightarrow 0 \] (the interval length halves at each step; compare Exercise 8.3). By Corollary 8.1, \((a_n)\) and \((b_n)\) converge to a common limit \(c\in[a,b]\).

Since \(f\) is continuous, Proposition 10.2 gives \(f(a_n)\to f(c)\) and \(f(b_n)\to f(c)\). Now \(f(a_n)\le0\) for every \(n\); if we had \(f(c)>0\), taking \(\varepsilon=f(c)/2\) in the definition of convergence would force \(f(a_n)>f(c)/2>0\) for large \(n\), a contradiction. So \(f(c)\le0\). The same argument with \(f(b_n)\ge0\) gives \(f(c)\ge0\). Hence \(f(c)=0\), and \(a<c<b\) since \(f(a)<0\) and \(f(b)>0\) rule out \(c=a\) or \(c=b\).

Remark 10.4. The same bisection idea proves the more general statement: if \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\) and \(y\) lies between \(f(a)\) and \(f(b)\), then \(f(c)=y\) for some \(c\in[a,b]\) — apply Theorem 10.2 to \(f(x)-y\).

10.8 Summary

  • \(\displaystyle\lim_{x\to a} f(x) = L\) (Definition 10.1) means \(f(x_n)\to L\) for every sequence \(x_n\to a\) in \(\operatorname{dom}(f)\) — this reduces function limits to sequence limits, and is equivalent to the \(\varepsilon\)–\(\delta\) condition of Proposition 10.1.
  • Continuity restated: \(f\) is continuous at \(a\) exactly when \(\lim_{x\to a}f(x)\) exists and equals \(f(a)\) (Proposition 10.2).
  • One-sided limits (Definition 10.2) test only monotone sequences approaching from one side. A jump discontinuity (Definition 10.3) is exactly a point where both one-sided limits exist (finitely) but disagree; \(f\) is continuous at \(a\) iff both one-sided limits equal \(f(a)\) (Theorem 10.1).
  • The limit laws (Proposition 10.3) compute limits of sums, products, and quotients termwise, and give continuity of \(f+g\), \(f\cdot g\), \(f/g\) from continuity of \(f\) and \(g\) (Corollary 10.1) — but a \(\tfrac00\) form must be simplified before the quotient rule applies (Example 10.6).
  • Compositions of continuous functions are continuous (Corollary 10.2), and more generally a limit may be passed through a continuous function from the outside in (Proposition 10.4). Together with the arithmetic rule above, this shows every elementary function is continuous where defined (Remark 10.3).
  • The Intermediate Value Theorem (Theorem 10.2): a continuous function that is negative at \(a\) and positive at \(b\) must vanish somewhere in between. The proof bisects \([a,b]\) into nested intervals shrinking to a point \(c\), then uses continuity to pass \(f(a_n)\le0\le f(b_n)\) to the limit.
