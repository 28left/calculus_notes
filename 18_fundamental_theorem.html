22  The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus

So far, integration and differentiation have been developed as two separate theories. The integral was defined through limits of Riemann sums (areas), the derivative through limits of difference quotients (slopes). The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus shows that the two operations are inverse to each other. This is the basic fact of calculus, and it turns the computation of integrals, which is hard to do directly from Riemann sums, into the problem of reversing a differentiation.

22.1 The Derivative of the Indefinite Integral

Recall that for a continuous function \(f\) on an interval \(I\) and a fixed \(\alpha\in I\), the indefinite integral of \(f\) is the function \[ \Phi(x) = \int_\alpha^x f(u)\,du \qquad (x\in I) \] (Definition 14.1). We know that \(\Phi\) is continuous (Theorem 14.1). In fact, much more is true.

Theorem 22.1 (Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, Part 1) Let \(f\) be continuous on an interval \(I\) and let \(\alpha\in I\). Then the indefinite integral \[ \Phi(x) = \int_\alpha^x f(u)\,du \] is differentiable at every \(x\in I\), and \[ \Phi'(x) = f(x), \qquad\text{that is,}\qquad \frac{d}{dx}\int_\alpha^x f(u)\,du = f(x). \] (If \(x\) is an endpoint of \(I\), \(\Phi'(x)\) is the one-sided derivative. The most important case is \(f\colon\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\), where this does not arise.)

NoteProof

Proof. Let \(x\in I\), and let \(h\neq0\) with \(x+h\in I\). By Proposition 14.1 and the Mean Value Theorem for Integrals (Theorem 13.1; for \(h<0\) use Remark 13.4, part 3), \[ \Phi(x+h) - \Phi(x) = \int_x^{x+h} f(u)\,du = h\cdot f(\xi) \] for some \(\xi\) between \(x\) and \(x+h\). Here \(f(\xi)\) is the average value of \(f\) on the interval between \(x\) and \(x+h\) (Definition 13.2). Dividing by \(h\), \[ \frac{\Phi(x+h)-\Phi(x)}{h} = f(\xi). \] The point \(\xi\) depends on \(h\), but it always lies between \(x\) and \(x+h\), so \(\lvert \xi-x \rvert\le\lvert h \rvert\). Hence, as \(h\to0\), \(\xi\to x\), and since \(f\) is continuous, \(f(\xi)\to f(x)\).

To make the last step precise, let \(\varepsilon>0\). Since \(f\) is continuous at \(x\) (Definition 6.2), there is \(\delta>0\) such that \(\lvert f(u)-f(x) \rvert<\varepsilon\) for all \(u\in I\) with \(\lvert u-x \rvert<\delta\). If \(0<\lvert h \rvert<\delta\), then \(\lvert \xi-x \rvert\le\lvert h \rvert<\delta\), and therefore \[ \lvert \frac{\Phi(x+h)-\Phi(x)}{h} - f(x) \rvert = \lvert f(\xi) - f(x) \rvert < \varepsilon. \] This shows \[ \lim_{h\to0}\frac{\Phi(x+h)-\Phi(x)}{h} = f(x), \] so \(\Phi\) is differentiable at \(x\) with \(\Phi'(x)=f(x)\) (Definition 15.2).

Geometrically, \(\Phi(x)\) is the (signed) area under the graph of \(f\) between \(\alpha\) and \(x\), and \(\Phi(x+h)-\Phi(x)\) is the area of the thin strip over the interval between \(x\) and \(x+h\). The Mean Value Theorem for Integrals replaces this strip by a rectangle of the same area, with width \(h\) and height \(f(\xi)\). The difference quotient is the height of that rectangle. As \(h\) shrinks, \(\xi\) is squeezed toward \(x\), and by continuity the height \(f(\xi)\) is pinned to \(f(x)\).

The interactive figure below shows this in two ways. In the left panel, the dashed coral rectangle over the interval between \(x\) and \(x+h\) has the same area as the blue strip, so its height is the difference quotient \(\frac{\Phi(x+h)-\Phi(x)}{h}\); the coral dot marks a point \(\xi\) with \(f(\xi)\) equal to that height. Remark 22.1. The third integrand in the figure shows why continuity is needed. It is the step function \[ f(u) = \begin{cases} 0.6 & u<1 \\ 1.6 & u\ge1, \end{cases} \] which jumps at \(u=1\). Integrating piece by piece from \(\alpha=0\) gives the piecewise linear function \(\Phi(x) = 0.6\,x\) for \(x\le1\) and \(\Phi(x) = 0.6+1.6\,(x-1)\) for \(x\ge1\). At \(x=1\), \[ \frac{\Phi(1+h)-\Phi(1)}{h} = \begin{cases} 1.6 & h>0, \\ 0.6 & h<0. \end{cases} \] The two one-sided limits differ, so \(\Phi\) has a corner at \(1\) and is not differentiable there. Whenever the strip straddles the jump, the figure finds no \(\xi\): the Mean Value Theorem for Integrals fails for discontinuous functions (Remark 13.4, part 2), and with it the proof of Theorem 22.1.

Since differentiable functions are continuous (Theorem 16.1), Theorem 22.1 also gives a second proof of Theorem 14.1.

22.2 Antiderivatives

Theorem 22.1 says that the indefinite integral solves the following problem: given \(f\), find a function whose derivative is \(f\). This problem, reversing differentiation, gets its own name.

Definition 22.1
Let \(f\) be a function on an interval \(I\). A function \(F\) is called an antiderivative (or primitive) of \(f\) on \(I\) if \(F\) is differentiable on \(I\) and \[ F'(x) = f(x) \qquad\text{for all } x\in I. \]

In this language, Theorem 22.1 reads:

Every indefinite integral of a continuous function \(f\) is an antiderivative of \(f\).

At first sight, finding antiderivatives looks like a problem of a quite different character from computing areas. That it is solved by the indefinite integral is what makes the theorem “fundamental”. But it leaves open a question.

Are there any other antiderivatives?

To answer it, we need one consequence of the Mean Value Theorem.

Proposition 22.1
Let \(f\) be continuous on \([a,b]\) and differentiable on \((a,b)\). If \(f'(x)=0\) for all \(x\in(a,b)\), then \(f\) is constant on \([a,b]\).

NoteProof

Proof. Let \(x,y\in[a,b]\) with \(x<y\). Then \(f\) is continuous on \([x,y]\) and differentiable on \((x,y)\), so by Theorem 18.1 there is \(\xi\in(x,y)\) with \[ f(y) - f(x) = f'(\xi)\,(y-x) = 0\cdot(y-x) = 0 . \] Hence \(f(x)=f(y)\). Since \(x\) and \(y\) were arbitrary, \(f(x)=f(a)\) for all \(x\in[a,b]\), so \(f\) is constant with value \(c=f(a)\).

Lemma 22.1
The difference between any two antiderivatives \(F_1\), \(F_2\) of \(f\) on an interval \(I\) is constant: \[ F_1(x) - F_2(x) = c \qquad\text{for all } x\in I. \]

NoteProof

Proof. Let \(G(x) := F_1(x) - F_2(x)\). By the sum and constant multiple rules for derivatives (Proposition 16.4, Proposition 16.5), \(G\) is differentiable on \(I\) with \[ G'(x) = F_1'(x) - F_2'(x) = f(x) - f(x) = 0 . \] Now let \(a<b\) in \(I\). On \([a,b]\), \(G\) is differentiable and hence continuous (Theorem 16.1), so Proposition 22.1 shows \(G(b)=G(a)\). Since \(a\) and \(b\) were arbitrary, \(G\) is constant on \(I\).

Remark 22.2. It matters that the domain is an interval. The function \[ G(x) = \begin{cases} 0 & x<0 \\ 1 & x>0 \end{cases} \] on \(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}\) has \(G'(x)=0\) at every point of its domain, but it is not constant. Consequently \(F_1(x)=-\frac1x\) and \(F_2(x)=-\frac1x+G(x)\) are both antiderivatives of \(\frac1{x^2}\) on \(\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}\), but their difference is not constant. The proof of Lemma 22.1 breaks down because there is no interval \([a,b]\) in the domain containing both a negative and a positive point.

The converse of Lemma 22.1 is easy.

Lemma 22.2
If \(F\) is an antiderivative of \(f\) and \(G(x) = F(x)+c\) for a constant \(c\), then \(G\) is also an antiderivative of \(f\).

NoteProof

Proof. By Proposition 16.4, and since every difference quotient of a constant function is \(0\), \[ G'(x) = \big(F(x)+c\big)' = F'(x) + \frac{d}{dx}\,c = F'(x) + 0 = f(x). \]

22.3 The Fundamental Theorem

Combining Theorem 22.1 with the two lemmas, we can describe all antiderivatives of a continuous function.

Theorem 22.2 (Fundamental Theorem of Calculus) Let \(f\) be continuous on an interval \(I\). Every antiderivative \(F\) of \(f\) can be represented in the form \[ F(x) = c + \Phi(x) = c + \int_\alpha^x f(u)\,du, \qquad (\ast) \] where \(c\) is a constant and \(\alpha\in I\). Conversely, for any choice of the constant \(c\) and of \(\alpha\in I\), the expression \((\ast)\) yields an antiderivative of \(f\).

NoteProof

Proof. Fix \(\alpha\in I\). By Theorem 22.1, \(\Phi(x)=\int_\alpha^x f(u)\,du\) is an antiderivative of \(f\). If \(F\) is any antiderivative of \(f\), then \(F-\Phi\) is constant by Lemma 22.1, say \(F-\Phi=c\), and so \(F = c+\Phi\).

Conversely, for any \(\alpha\in I\) the function \(\Phi(x) = \int_\alpha^x f(u)\,du\) is an antiderivative of \(f\) by Theorem 22.1, and so is \(c+\Phi\) for every constant \(c\), by Lemma 22.2.

Remark 22.3. Changing the lower limit \(\alpha\) only changes \(\Phi\) by an additive constant (Proposition 14.1). One might therefore guess that the constant \(c\) in \((\ast)\) is superfluous, that is, that every antiderivative is an indefinite integral \(\int_\alpha^x f(u)\,du\) for a suitable \(\alpha\). Sometimes this is true. The antiderivatives of \(f(x)=x^2\) are the functions \(\frac{x^3}{3}+c\), and since \(\int_\alpha^x u^2\,du = \frac{x^3}{3}-\frac{\alpha^3}{3}\), the choice \(\alpha = \sqrt[3]{-3c}\) produces each of them. But in general it is false. For \(f(x)=0\), every indefinite integral is \(\int_\alpha^x 0\,du = 0\), whatever \(\alpha\) is, while every constant function is an antiderivative of \(f\). So the constant \(c\) cannot be dropped.

22.4 Evaluating Definite Integrals

The most important application of the Fundamental Theorem is the evaluation of definite integrals \[ \int_a^b f(u)\,du . \] Suppose we know one antiderivative \(F\) of \(f\). Let \(\Phi(x) = \int_a^x f(u)\,du\) be the indefinite integral with lower limit \(a\). Both \(F\) and \(\Phi\) are antiderivatives of \(f\) (the latter by Theorem 22.1), so by Lemma 22.1, \[ F(x) = \Phi(x) + c \] for some constant \(c\). Since \[ \Phi(a) = \int_a^a f(u)\,du = 0 \] (Definition 13.1), we get \(F(a) = 0+c\), that is, \(c=F(a)\). Hence \(\Phi(x) = F(x) - F(a)\), and in particular \(\int_a^b f(u)\,du = \Phi(b) = F(b) - F(a)\).

Corollary 22.1 (Evaluation of definite integrals) Let \(f\) be continuous on an interval \(I\), let \(a,b\in I\), and let \(F\) be any antiderivative of \(f\) on \(I\). Then \[ \int_a^b f(u)\,du = F(b) - F(a). \]

Any antiderivative works! Compare this with Proposition 14.1, part 1, which gave the same formula for an indefinite integral \(\Phi\) in place of \(F\). There, we first had to compute \(\Phi\), which means computing integrals. Now any function whose derivative is \(f\) will do, and such functions can often be found by reading formulas for derivatives backwards. Note also that \(a<b\) is not required: the formula holds for all \(a,b\in I\).

Notation. The difference \(F(b)-F(a)\) is written \[ F(b) - F(a) = F(x)\Big|_a^b = \Big[F(x)\Big]_a^b . \] For an antiderivative of \(f\) one writes \[ \int f(x)\,dx = F(x) + c, \] without limits of integration, and calls it the indefinite integral of \(f\) as well.

Remark 22.4. This “indefinite” indefinite integral needs some care. By Theorem 22.2, \(\int f(x)\,dx\) stands for some antiderivative of \(f\), determined only up to the additive constant \(c\); the formula \(\int f(x)\,dx = F(x)+c\) is just a symbolic way of writing \(F'(x) = f(x)\). Strictly speaking, it is also slightly inconsistent to use the same letter \(x\) for the variable of integration and for the independent variable of \(F\). And it is worth remembering that, a priori, integrating (computing areas by limits of Riemann sums) and reversing differentiation are two different things. It is only the Fundamental Theorem that justifies using the name “indefinite integral” for both.

Example 22.1
(a) The function \(F(x) = x^3 - x^2\) has \(F'(x) = 3x^2 - 2x\), so \[ \int_1^2 \big(3x^2 - 2x\big)\,dx = \Big[x^3 - x^2\Big]_1^2 = (8-4) - (1-1) = 4 . \]

  1. Since \((-\cos x)' = \sin x\), \[ \int_0^\pi \sin x\,dx = \Big[-\cos x\Big]_0^\pi = -\cos\pi + \cos 0 = 1 + 1 = 2 . \] This agrees with the average value \(\frac2\pi\) of \(\sin\) on \([0,\pi]\) found in Example 13.5.

In the same way, each formula in Proposition 13.4 now follows from Corollary 22.1 as soon as we know the derivative of the function on the right-hand side: no Riemann sums are needed anymore.

22.5 Summary

  • FTC, Part 1 (Theorem 22.1): if \(f\) is continuous, the indefinite integral \(\Phi(x)=\int_\alpha^x f(u)\,du\) is differentiable with \(\Phi'=f\). The proof combines the Mean Value Theorem for Integrals, \(\frac{\Phi(x+h)-\Phi(x)}{h} = f(\xi)\) with \(\xi\) between \(x\) and \(x+h\), with the continuity of \(f\). Without continuity the conclusion can fail (Remark 22.1).
  • An antiderivative (or primitive) of \(f\) is a function \(F\) with \(F'=f\) (Definition 22.1). Every indefinite integral of a continuous function is one.
  • If \(f'=0\) on an interval, then \(f\) is constant (Proposition 22.1, from the Mean Value Theorem). Hence two antiderivatives of the same function on an interval differ by a constant (Lemma 22.1), and adding a constant to an antiderivative gives another one (Lemma 22.2). On domains that are not intervals, this fails (Remark 22.2).
  • Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (Theorem 22.2): the antiderivatives of a function \(f\) that is continuous on an interval are exactly the functions \(c + \int_\alpha^x f(u)\,du\). The constant \(c\) cannot be dropped in general (Remark 22.3).
  • Evaluation of definite integrals (Corollary 22.1): for any antiderivative \(F\) of \(f\), \(\int_a^b f(u)\,du = F(b)-F(a) = \big[F(x)\big]_a^b\). The notation \(\int f(x)\,dx = F(x)+c\) stands for an arbitrary antiderivative (Remark 22.4).
